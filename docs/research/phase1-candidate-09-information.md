**Status:** v1 audit, September 2026. A practitioner audit and a Final Evidence & Falsification Gate are expected to follow.

> ### ⚠️ ACCESS LIMITATION
> - **No full text was accessible.** Publishers, PMC pages, federation documents, podcasts and coaching sites were refused by the network proxy.
> - All claims rest on **abstracts (AO)** or **search-index summaries (SIO)**. **UNR** = unresolved detail.
> - Effect sizes are quoted **as indexed**; several need checking against the originals (flagged).

> **Central question.** What information must players perceive to solve a game problem, and what changes when the coach adds, removes, highlights, substitutes or delays that information?
>
> **Taxonomy question.** Is INFORMATION a practice-design property, a coaching / intervention method, several concepts, or an unhelpful catch-all?
>
> **Frozen coach-facing question:** *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"*
>
> **CF-1 v2:** *behaviour from a supplied condition is not evidence players can make the decisions that condition made for them. Evidence about decisions still available to them is unaffected. This limits inference; it does not judge the practice.*
>
> **Chain kept separate throughout:**
> INFORMATION AVAILABLE → ATTENDED TO → USED → DECISION / ACTION → PRACTICE PERFORMANCE → LEARNING → RETENTION → TRANSFER.
>
> **Preserved:** supplying information is **not** inherently bad coaching. The question is what the information does to the problem players are asked to solve.

## 0. Honest headline

1. **"Information" names at least two different things.** What the **game** makes available to perceive, and what the **coach** supplies (instructions, cues, demonstrations, questions, feedback). They behave differently and are studied by different traditions.
2. **Changing the game information changes the task, not just its difficulty.** Removing a bowler's action (ball machine), swapping an opponent for a light, or moving from video to on-field all change how players coordinate and where they look. **SUPPORTED (acute).**
3. **Perceptual training works in the lab and partly on the field.** Video / occlusion training improves anticipation with sizeable effects; the gain on **real-game** measures is roughly half the lab gain in team-sport studies. **Competition transfer: weakly established.**
4. **How attention is guided matters, and not always as expected.** In one handball study, verbal cueing and explicit rules helped; **colour highlighting did not**. In one tennis study, explicit rules sped learning but **held up worse under anxiety**. Small, novice, lab-based.
5. **Several famous motor-learning "rules" have shrunk.** The guidance hypothesis (frequent feedback → dependency), external focus and self-controlled practice all show **small or negligible effects after bias correction** in recent meta-analyses.
6. **Substitution** (a coach signal replaces the game information that should trigger an action) is **not an established term**, but its foundations are: generic stimuli (lights) do not reproduce the problem posed by an opponent's movement.
7. **Taxonomy (§40): C, split.** GAME INFORMATION is a property of the problem (mostly produced by other levers; small residue). COACH-SUPPLIED INFORMATION is a separate family of help. **Feedback** differs in timing and function and is flagged to split out.

## 1. Defining "information": how traditions use the word

| Term | Tradition | Meaning | PáircVision status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Game information** (ecological information) | Ecological psychology (Gibson); ecological dynamics | Structure in the environment (optic flow, opponent movement, gaps) that **specifies or correlates with** opportunities for action | Core of "what players must perceive" |
| **Perceptual information** | All traditions | What reaches the senses and can be picked up | Broad umbrella; not a category |
| **Specifying vs non-specifying information** | Ecological psychology (Jacobs & Michaels; Fajen) | Specifying variables reliably specify a property; non-specifying ones correlate only in some conditions. Learners often start with non-specifying variables and shift with practice (**attunement / education of attention**) | Scientifically warranted in lab tasks (e.g., braking, haptic length); **not demonstrated for invasion-game tactics** |
| **Contextual information / priors** | Cognitive anticipation research (Gredin, Williams, Broadbent; Loffing & Cañal-Bruland) | Non-kinematic information: opponent tendencies, score, match situation, previous actions | Real and measurable (§11) |
| **Advance information** | Anticipation research | Information available **before** a key event (e.g., opponent's posture before the kick) | A time-stamped subset of game information |
| **Coach instruction** | Pedagogy; motor learning | Verbal statements about what to do or how | Coach-supplied |
| **Cues / prompts** | Motor learning; coaching | Short words or signals directing attention or action | Coach-supplied; function varies (§5, §7) |
| **Demonstration / modelling** | Observational learning (Bandura; Ste-Marie) | Showing an action or a solution | Coach-supplied, usually **before** action |
| **Questioning** | TGfU / Game Sense / guided discovery | Prompts asking players to identify or explain | Coach-supplied; supplies **some** of the answer (§16) |
| **Augmented feedback** | Motor learning (Schmidt; Salmoni et al.) | Information **about** an action from an external source | Coach-supplied, **after / during** action |
| **Knowledge of results (KR)** | Motor learning | About the outcome ("wide left") | Feedback subtype |
| **Knowledge of performance (KP)** | Motor learning | About the movement pattern ("your hips opened early") | Feedback subtype |
| **Visual / auditory information** | All | Modality | A property of any of the above, not a category |

**Finding.** The traditions do **not** agree on one meaning. Collapsing them under one word would hide the key difference: **information in the game players must learn to use** vs **information the coach adds to help**.

## 2. Game information vs coach information

| GAME PROVIDES | Source | Available in competition? |
|---|---|---|
| Opponent body orientation; defender distance; teammate movement; open space; ball flight; target location; numerical relationship | The game itself | **Yes** |
| Score / time state | The game (scoreboard, clock, referee) | **Yes**, with varying salience |
| Teammate calls ("man on") | Players | **Yes** (§21) |

| COACH PROVIDES | When | Available in competition? |
|---|---|---|
| "Press now" (call) | During | Partly (sideline calls exist; much is inaudible) |
| "Look for his hips" (attentional cue) | Before / during | No (as a live prompt) |
| "Nearest player presses" (relational instruction) | Before | As a **learned rule**, yes |
| Coloured cue; whistle | During | **No** |
| Freeze-and-explain | Paused | No |
| Question | Paused / after | No |
| Video / diagram before practice | Before | No (as a live aid) |

**Do they belong in the same category?**
- **Differently:** game information is **what the problem is made of**; coach information is **help with, or substitutes for, parts of the problem**.
- **But they interact:** a coach cue works *through* game information (it directs attention to it), replaces it (a whistle instead of a trigger), or adds to it (a prior about the opponent).
- **Provisional answer: not the same category.** They need a shared boundary rule (§40), not a shared box.

## 3. Representative information

| Evidence | Finding | Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Pinder, Davids & Renshaw** (2009; 2011): cricket batting vs live bowler, ball machine, video | Removing the bowler's action (ball machine) **delayed movement initiation**, reduced peak bat speed and **reduced bat–ball contact quality**. A life-size video of the bowler preserved the **initial** response pattern | Acute (AO) |
| **Dicks, Button & Davids (2010)**, penalty goalkeepers, video vs in situ | Gaze differed by task: with limited movement responses, keepers fixated more on the **kicker's body**; when actually trying to save, gaze was split between **body and ball** | Acute (AO) |
| **Mann, Williams, Ward & Janelle (2007)** meta-analysis (42 studies) | Experts outperform non-experts in response accuracy and time; expertise effects are **moderated by paradigm and stimulus presentation** | Cross-sectional (AO) |
| **Reactive agility** (Farrow, Young & Bruce 2005; Young & Farrow 2013) | Responding to an **opponent's movement** discriminates skill levels (netball, AFL, rugby league); responding to a **generic light** does not | Cross-sectional / test design (AO / SIO) |
| **Pinder et al. (2011)**, representative learning design | Practice should **sample** performance information and keep information–action couplings | **Theory** (AO) |

**What must remain available to preserve a competition decision?**
- **Supported in principle:** the information that normally **specifies or predicts** the opportunity (opponent kinematics, spacing, ball flight), and the **coupling** between that information and a real action.
- **Theory ≠ transfer evidence.** These studies show **tasks change** when information changes. None shows that **more representative information produces better transfer** in an invasion game.

## 4. Remove information

| Manipulation | Evidence | Acute vs learning |
|---|---|---|
| **Temporal occlusion video training** | Meta-analysis (*Sports Med* 2024): **large** training effect; **no difference** between video and field-based **transfer tests** | Learning on representative tests (AO). Field-based tests ≠ competition |
| **Point-light displays** (removing superficial information) | Ryu, Abernethy, Park & Mann (2018): novice badminton players (n = 36) improved anticipation of **deceptive** actions; held at 1-week retention | **Learning + short retention** (AO); racket sport; novices |
| **Blurred video** (referees) | Improved decision-making of skilled referees (PMC6170623; SIO) | Learning (officials, not players) |
| **Stroboscopic glasses** | Meta-analyses: moderate effects on reaction-time and accuracy **tests**; "far transfer" to sport performance **insufficiently supported** | Near transfer only |
| **Ball machine** (removes bowler information) | Changed coordination (Pinder) | **Acute: different task** |
| **Hiding opponent / masking cues** in live invasion games | **No study located** | UNKNOWN |

**Does removing information train players to perceive better, or create a different task?**
- **Both, depending on how.**
  - Removing **irrelevant or deceptive surface information** in training (point-light) can **improve** pickup of the information that matters. (Racket sport, novices, lab.)
  - Removing **task-relevant information during practice of the action** (ball machine) **creates a different task** with different coordination.
  - Occlusion as a **training tool** improves anticipation tests; as a **practice condition** in live games it is unstudied.
- **"Removing visual information improves perception": PARTIALLY SUPPORTED only for specific designs, populations and tests.**

## 5. Add information

| Added information | Evidence |
|---|---|
| **Verbal direction to key cues** | Abernethy, Schorer, Jackson & Hagemann (2012), handball goalkeeping, 60 novices: verbal cueing produced **sustained improvement** |
| **Colour highlighting of cues** | Same study: colour cueing performed **no better than controls** |
| **Explicit rules** ("if X, then Y") | Same study: sustained improvement. Smeeton et al. (2005, tennis): faster acquisition, but **slower responses under anxiety** than guided-discovery or discovery groups |
| **Lights / generic reaction cues** (e.g., light-based reaction systems) | Improve reaction to lights; **do not** reproduce opponent-movement demands (reactive agility literature) |
| **Explicit contextual priors** (opponent tendencies) | Gredin et al. (2018): improved anticipation **efficiency** in experts; biased attention and expectation in experts, not novices (§11) |
| **Instruction added to a game** | *RQES* 2024 (youth soccer, 12–15 y, n = 20): instruction associated with more **connected passing networks**; constraints without instruction associated with more **individual actions** |

**Can added information guide attention without replacing game information?**
- **Yes, when it points to the game information** (verbal cueing of where to look), per the handball study.
- **Not automatically**: a **salient artificial marker** (colour) did not help. It may draw attention to the marker rather than teach players to find the cue without it (interpretation; UNR mechanism).

**What happens when the cue disappears?**
- In the handball study, tests were **without cues**; verbal cueing and explicit rules still showed benefits. So attention guidance **can** leave something behind **in lab anticipation tasks**.
- **Invasion-game evidence with cue removal: none located. UNKNOWN.**

## 6. Substitute information

**Example:** real trigger (receiver turned away, heavy touch) → practice trigger (coach whistle).

| Parallel evidence | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Reactive agility: light vs opponent | A generic signal **does not pose the same perceptual problem**; skill differences disappear |
| Ball machine vs bowler | Replacing the natural information source **changes timing and coordination** |
| Video vs in situ (Dicks) | Changing the response and information source changes **where players look** |
| GAME MOMENT gate: coach-called vs game-generated turnover | Changes whether **recognising** the moment is part of the task |
| DIRECTION gate: target switched by coach call | Classified as INFORMATION (signal source) |

**Status of "information substitution":**
- **Not an established term** in the literature searched.
- **PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS on supported foundations.** The components (task changes when information source changes; generic stimuli don't pose the specific problem) are **SUPPORTED (acute)**.
- **Learning consequence** (players trained on a whistle fail to recognise the real trigger): **UNKNOWN**, logically expected.
- **Applies CF-1 v2 directly:** a whistle supplies **WHEN** (and often WHAT); pressing after it is not evidence of recognising the trigger.

## 7. Highlight information

| Coach says | Function (lens) |
|---|---|
| "Press!" | Supplies **WHAT + WHEN** (the decision) |
| "Watch when the receiver can't see forward." | Supplies **what to attend to**; leaves detection, interpretation and action choice partly open |
| "What tells you that you can press?" | Asks the player to **name** the information; supplies the *topic* |

**Evidence:**
- **Verbal cueing to key information** can improve anticipation (handball; tennis, Williams et al. 2002).
- **Explicit versus guided discovery** (Smeeton et al. 2005): both faster than pure discovery; explicit **less robust under anxiety**. Guided discovery gave the best balance **in that study**.
- **Attentional focus** (internal vs external): the external-focus advantage is **negligible after bias correction** (McKay et al., 2024, *Psychol Bull*). Not a tactical-cueing finding in any case (§18).
- **Education of attention** (ecological): practice **with feedback** shifts learners toward more useful variables in lab tasks.

**Verdict.** Highlighting information and supplying the decision are **different functions**: **SUPPORTED as a distinction** (logic + DA-1), with **PARTIAL** empirical support that pointing to information can improve perception. **"Highlighting is always better": not supported.** Explicit highlighting can be fragile under pressure (one study).

## 8. Perceptual cues in invasion sports

| Cue | Evidence (sport) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Centre of mass / global body motion** | Rugby side-step: skilled players less susceptible to deception; detection correlates with the attacker's centre-of-mass motion (Jackson, Warren & Abernethy 2006; Brault et al.) | Experts appear to use **global** kinematics |
| **Head, shoulders, hips** (deceptive vs honest cues) | Rugby / soccer deception literature: head and upper-trunk movements can be **deceptive**; lower-trunk / centre-of-mass more honest (SIO) | Coaching "watch the hips" has **some** basis; **not** a single correct cue |
| **Kicker's body vs ball** | Penalty goalkeepers (Dicks 2010) | Task-dependent |
| **Teammate / opponent positions** | Soccer scanning (Aksum et al. 2021): scan stop-points are information-rich; more opponents than teammates in view | Correlational; n = 4 |
| **Handball throw** | Goalkeeper anticipation (Schorer, Abernethy) | Trainable in lab |
| **Contextual probabilities** | Soccer (Gredin et al.) | Experts integrate priors |
| **Gaelic / hurling** | Focus groups: senior players integrate positioning, space, score, time, instructions (Frontiers 2023); hurling inter-county players better on **coincidence anticipation** than club players (SIO) | **No cue-specific Gaelic study** |

**What experts use ≠ what coaches should tell novices.**
- Experts' cue use is often **not verbalisable**, relies on **global** patterns, and is integrated with context.
- Telling novices one cue may help early (verbal cueing) but can **mislead** if the cue is deceptive or context-dependent, and explicit rules can be fragile under anxiety.
- **Status:** the distinction is **SUPPORTED** (logic + expertise research). **Which cue to tell which player: UNKNOWN.**

## 9. Anticipation

- **Expert–novice differences:** robust (Mann et al. 2007): better accuracy and speed; fewer, longer fixations; moderated by paradigm.
- **Sources:** advance postural cues, pattern recognition, contextual priors, opponent tendencies.
- **Can it be trained by manipulating information?**
  - **Lab / video:** yes (temporal occlusion meta-analysis 2024, large effects; team-sport PCT meta-analysis 2024).
  - **Field-based tests:** yes in some studies (Williams et al. 2002, tennis; occlusion meta-analysis: no difference video vs field tests).
  - **Real-game performance (team sports):** **ES ≈ 0.65 vs ≈ 1.51 in the lab** (PCT meta-analysis, 22 studies, 45 effect sizes; elite athletes; AO).
  - **Competition transfer in invasion sports:** **thin**; mostly single studies without long retention.
- **Separation kept:** video test ≠ live task ≠ competition.

## 10. Scanning

**Carried from TIME gate:** less scanning under tight pressure (association); scanning training increased scans (elite only) **without improving next-action success** (Hintermann et al., 2025).

**What INFORMATION adds:**
- **Aksum et al. (2021)**, 11v11 eye-tracking, 4 elite midfielders: **fixations occurred in only 2.3 % of scans**; the **stop point** of a scan is the information-rich part.
  - A scan is a head movement; **whether and what information is extracted is unclear**.
- **"No evidence that visual exploratory activity distinguishes the super elite from elite"** (2024; SIO).
- **Ice hockey (2024):** scanning associated with better performance (association).
- **Imagery intervention** increased scanning in elite academy players (Pocock et al., 2019).

**Rejected chain:** MORE SCANS → BETTER PERCEPTION → BETTER DECISION.
- Link 1 (more scans → more information **available**): plausible.
- Link 2 (→ information **attended to / used**): **not shown**; fixations rare.
- Link 3 (→ better **decisions**): **association only**; training did not improve outcomes.
- **Status: CONTRADICTED as a causal chain** by the one intervention; each link needs its own evidence.

## 11. Contextual information and score / time state

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Gredin et al. (2018; 2020) soccer anticipation | Explicit priors about opponent tendencies improved anticipation efficiency; biased judgements toward the likely action; effects depend on task load and prior reliability |
| Bayesian integration framework (Gredin et al. 2020 review) | Priors and kinematics are weighted by reliability (theory + lab) |
| Known risk (anticipation literature; UNR detail) | When the actual action **contradicts** the prior, performance suffers |
| Gaelic focus groups (2023) | Players report using **score and time remaining**, pre-match instructions and opposition status |

**Where does score / time state belong?**

| Candidate home | Fit |
|---|---|
| INFORMATION | Players **perceive and use** it; whether it is displayed or announced is an information-availability choice |
| GAME MOMENT | A coach **selects** a scenario ("2 down, 3 minutes left"): that is choosing the problem |
| SCORING | Only the **values** of scores; not the state |
| TIME | Only a **clock / deadline** manipulation; "3 minutes left" as a story is not a clock |
| A new GAME STATE concept | Would duplicate GAME MOMENT's scenario role |

**Recommendation (not frozen):**
- **Selecting** a score / time scenario → **GAME MOMENT** (choosing the problem).
- **Running** an actual countdown → **TIME**.
- **Whether players can see / are told the state** → **game-information availability** (§40).
- **No GAME STATE category.** Do not force score / time into INFORMATION wholesale.

## 12. Body orientation

| Use | Home |
|---|---|
| A **starting** orientation set by the coach (receive back-to-goal) | **GAME MOMENT** configuration (initial value) |
| Its **effect**: what the player can see; the angle to target and defenders | **Game-information availability** + SPACE / DIRECTION (relational) |
| An **opponent's** orientation as a trigger ("he's facing his own goal") | **Game information** (a cue players read) |
| A player's **receiving orientation / open body shape** as a skill | **Execution (HOW)**, not a manipulation. If the coach **requires** it, **ACTIONS** |

**Resolved:** body orientation is **several things depending on use**. It does not justify a category. The GAME MOMENT gate's provisional home (INFORMATION) is **refined**: starting orientation = GAME MOMENT configuration; its consequence = information availability.

## 13. Instruction mapped through Decision Allocation

| Instruction | Information supplied | Decisions supplied (lens) | Decisions remaining |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Press." | Action + moment | **WHAT, WHEN** (often WHO) | HOW (angle, speed) |
| "Press when he receives backwards." | A **rule** linking a cue to an action | WHAT; **the trigger to use** | **Detecting** the cue; WHO; HOW |
| "Watch his body shape." | **Where to attend** | Attention target | Interpretation; WHETHER / WHEN / HOW |
| "What tells you that you can press?" | The **topic** (pressing cues exist) | Very little; often implies there is a cue | Identifying the cue; all action decisions |
| No instruction | — | — | Everything, including what to attend to |

**Scientific stress-test:**
- Candidate 07's claim, "instructions are information; Decision Allocation describes which decisions an instruction has already specified", **holds**.
- **Nuance added:** instructions can supply **attention targets** and **if–then rules**, not only decisions. A rule ("press when he receives backwards") leaves **detection** alive but supplies the **criterion**. CF-1 v2 then says: pressing well after the rule is evidence of **detecting and executing**, not of **discovering the criterion**.

## 14. Feedback

| Aspect | Evidence |
|---|---|
| KR / KP | Established distinctions (Schmidt tradition) |
| Frequency (reduced vs 100 %) | Meta-analysis (*Psychol Sport Exerc* 2022): reduced frequency shows only a **marginal** benefit on delayed retention (g ≈ .19); **no acquisition-to-retention reversal**; the guidance hypothesis "is not supported by the extant research"; studies underpowered |
| Faded feedback | Winstein & Schmidt (1990): fading supported learning in the original experiments; later evidence mixed |
| Bandwidth feedback | Established technique; effect size in team sports **UNR** |
| Self-controlled feedback | Bias-corrected effect **g ≈ 0.11** (McKay et al. 2022 / 2023) |
| Concurrent vs terminal | Concurrent can boost performance during practice and lose it at retention (review summaries); task-dependent |
| Delayed feedback | Limited sport-specific evidence (UNR) |
| Team-sport tactical feedback | **Very little experimental evidence** |

**Should INFORMATION be split?**
- **Information for the action** (what a player can use to decide and act) and **information about the action** (feedback) differ in **timing**, **source**, **purpose** and **research tradition**.
- Feedback is a **coaching process** applied after or during performance; it does **not** change the game problem.
- **Recommendation:** **yes, flag the split.** Feedback belongs to a coach-intervention family, not to game information. Final placement: gate.

## 15. Guidance hypothesis

- **Claim:** frequent or concurrent augmented information improves practice performance but creates **dependency**, hurting retention (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter 1984).
- **Evidence now:**
  - Original lab experiments (e.g., Winstein & Schmidt 1990) supported reduced / faded frequency.
  - **2022 meta-analysis:** evidence for the guidance hypothesis is **weak**; no reversal; marginal retention benefit; underpowered literature.
  - Concurrent feedback: dependency effects reported in some lab tasks (reviews; SIO).
- **Population / task:** almost entirely **simple motor tasks** in lab settings with young adults.
- **Transfer to Gaelic tactical learning:** **not warranted.** At most, a **hypothesis** that constant coach calls could create reliance on calls (§6 substitution is the stronger argument).
- **Status: "supplied information creates dependency": PARTIALLY SUPPORTED at best (motor tasks); UNKNOWN for tactics.**

## 16. Questions

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Coach questioning in youth soccer (Cope, Partington et al. 2016; "The art of the question" 2022) | ~71 questions per session; ~52 % convergent (closed); earlier studies ~87 % convergent. Descriptive only |
| Práxedes et al. (2016), questioning-based programme, young footballers | Improved decisions in passing and dribbling vs control (preliminary; small) |
| TGfU vs direct instruction RCT (U-12, n = 30) | TGfU (incl. questioning) better decision-making (AO) |
| TGfU meta-analysis (13 RCTs) | Reported SMD 5.93 for decision-making: **implausibly large; treat as UNR** |
| Gaelic GBA intervention (*RQES* 2025) | Decision-making variables improved; players were **less receptive to player huddles** |
| Guided discovery (Smeeton 2005) | Balanced speed and robustness (anticipation, tennis) |

**Does asking preserve decisions better than telling?**
- **No clean evidence isolates questioning.** TGfU / GBA packages bundle modified games, sequencing and questioning. Effects cannot be attributed to questions.
- **A leading ("convergent") question can supply almost as much as an instruction.** The majority of coach questions are convergent.
- **Status: UNKNOWN.** "Questioning is always better than telling": **UNSUPPORTED**.

## 17. Explicit instruction

| Potential benefit | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Accelerates acquisition | Explicit and guided groups learned faster than discovery (Smeeton 2005); explicit rules improved handball anticipation (Abernethy 2012) |
| Coordinates teams | Instruction associated with more connected passing (*RQES* 2024) |
| Teaches technique | Broad motor-learning literature (demonstration + instruction); effect sizes vary |
| Reduces search space | Logical; consistent with the above |

| Potential cost | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Fragile under pressure | Explicit group slower under anxiety (Smeeton 2005; one study) |
| Supplies decisions players must later make | CF-1 v2 (inference, not harm) |
| Reduced exploration / individual action | *RQES* 2024: without instruction, more individual actions (could be good or bad) |

**When each matters (PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS):** instruction is most clearly useful when **coordination or execution** is the objective or when learners are early; its cost matters when the **recognition decision** is the objective. **"Coach instruction harms learning": UNSUPPORTED.**

## 18. External vs internal focus

- **Classic claim:** an external focus (on the effect of movement) beats an internal focus (on body parts) for performance and learning (Wulf; OPTIMAL theory).
- **Now:** a robust Bayesian meta-analysis (McKay et al., 2024, *Psychological Bulletin*) found **moderate-to-strong publication bias** and **negligible** bias-corrected effects: g ≈ 0.01 (performance), 0.15 (retention), 0.09 (transfer).
- **Tactical relevance:** none demonstrated; the literature concerns **movement execution**.
- **Placement:** **not INFORMATION as game-problem design.** Save for later **coaching-intervention** material, with the bias caveat.

---

## 19. Demonstration, video, diagrams

| Evidence | Finding |
|---|---|
| Observational learning reviews (Ste-Marie et al.) | Demonstrations convey movement and strategy information; effectiveness depends on model, timing, learner (motor skills) |
| **Khacharem, Zoudji, Kalyuga et al.** (soccer / basketball tactical visualisations) | **Expertise reversal:** novices learn better from **static or segmented** presentations; experts can use **animations**. Arrows and sequencing help novices |
| Rekik et al. (2021) | Sequential arrows improved soccer knowledge from diagrams |
| Mguidich et al. (2026), handball | Static diagrams and imagination: differential effects for novices vs experts |
| Tactical recall vs on-field transfer | Studies measure **recall / comprehension**, not on-field decisions |

**Does showing the solution before practice help or reduce exploration?**
- **Helps understanding / recall**, with format depending on expertise (**SUPPORTED** for recall tasks).
- **Effect on exploration / recognition in play: UNKNOWN.** No study compared "shown solution first" vs "discover first" on in-game recognition.
- Flag (no design): Tactical Slate would sit in this evidence base; format × expertise matters; **field transfer is unmeasured**.

## 20. Before vs during vs after

| Timing | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Before** | Instruction, demonstration, video, diagram, priors | Sets up **what to look for / aim for** |
| **During** | Calls, cues, whistles, concurrent feedback, teammate talk | **Guides or substitutes** live perception and decision |
| **After** | KR / KP, questions, video review, freeze-and-explain | **Evaluates and explains** |

**Is one category too broad?**
- **Yes.** Timing changes the function:
  - **during-action** coach information can **substitute** for game information (whistle);
  - **before** information sets attention or supplies a plan;
  - **after** information is feedback.
- **Recommendation:** timing is a **necessary descriptor** inside coach-supplied information, and the **after** block (feedback) is the strongest candidate to split out.

## 21. Player-generated information

- A teammate's "man on" is **game information** from the players' side, not coach-supplied.
- Communication "underpins coordination" in team sports; in-game messages are short, task-focused; nonverbal signals common (soccer / team-cognition studies; SIO).
- **Ecological review** (Caso, Hill, Araújo & van der Kamp, 2026): communication serves **collective attention to information specifying shared affordances**. Theory, with a research agenda (AO).
- Team cognition (soccer, 2019): under time pressure, **shared knowledge** allows **implicit** coordination without talk.

**Does the framework need the distinction?** **Yes:** information **available in the game** (including player talk) vs information **deliberately supplied by the coach or design**. Player talk is part of the **game**; the coach's call is **help**.

**Stress-test of the architecture.**
- The precedence rule **works mechanically** (speaking is an action).
- **But** communication is an unusual action whose **main effect is informational**. The rule homes it in ACTIONS and records the information effect as secondary.
- **Edge case for the gate:** acceptable, or does "communication" need a note that its effect is on **shared information**, not on the ball game? **Recommendation:** keep ACTIONS primary; record the edge case. No study of silent games' learning effects was located.

## 23. Decision Allocation (integrated)

Applied throughout §13, §33. Key readings:
- "Press now" supplies **WHAT / WHEN** (often WHO).
- "Watch his body shape" supplies the **attention target**, leaving interpretation and action.
- A whistle supplies **WHEN** and removes **detection**.
- A diagram before practice supplies a **plan** (WHAT / WHERE), leaving timing and execution.
- Feedback after action supplies an **evaluation**, not a live decision.

**Not assumed:** that supplying only the attention target is always better. For coordination, safety or early learners, supplying WHAT / WHEN may be the point (DA-3; CF-1 v2).

## 24. CF-1 v2

**Applied:** if the coach calls every transition, players' transition behaviour does not show they recognised the moment. Their decisions **after** the call (angles, support, first pass) remain informative.

**Does INFORMATION need a CF-1 change?**
- **No amendment.** CF-1 v2 already lists "a called moment" among supplied conditions.
- **Clarification worth carrying:** an **attention cue** ("watch his hips") supplies *where to look*, not *what to decide*. Behaviour after it is evidence of **interpreting and acting on** that information, **not** of knowing where to look unprompted. That is CF-1 v2 applied to attention, not a change.

## 25. Perception → decision → action

| View | Position |
|---|---|
| Cognitive / information-processing | Serial stages: perceive, decide, act |
| Ecological dynamics | **Perception and action are coupled**; affordances are perceived relative to action capabilities; decision-making **emerges** in the performer–environment system; not a separate stage |
| Evidence both can cite | Video vs in situ gaze differs (Dicks 2010); ball machine changes coordination (Pinder); reactive agility needs opponent stimulus |

**Verdict.**
- The serial chain is **disputed** and, for fast invasion-game actions, **too linear**.
- **Usable coaching language without the theoretical claim:** *"what's there to see → what they pick up → what they do"* as **plain description**, never as a processing model.
- PáircVision should **not** build on a cognitive-processing model. The internal evidence chain (§ header) is a **measurement ladder**, not a theory of mind.

## 26. "What must the player notice?"

**Candidate coaching question (not frozen).**

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Scientifically defensible? | **Partly.** Specific information sources matter (anticipation, cue research, representative design) |
| Problem 1: information is relational / dynamic | **Yes.** Experts use **global** motion and **relations** (spacing, timing), not one discrete cue |
| Problem 2: expert use is often non-conscious | **Yes.** "Notice" implies conscious detection; much pickup is not verbalisable |
| Problem 3: telling one cue can mislead | **Yes.** Deceptive cues (head / shoulders); explicit rules fragile under anxiety (one study) |
| Practically meaningful? | **Yes, for the coach's design thinking**: it forces the question "is the information that triggers this decision present in my practice?" |
| Risk | It invites coaches to **tell players the cue**, turning a design check into an instruction |

**Better internal form (PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS):** *"What in the game tells a player this is on, and is that still in my practice?"*
- This is a **design check on the practice**, not a question to ask players.
- **Not frozen.** Carried to the practitioner audit.

## 38. U12 volunteer translation

> **Before you shout the answer, ask yourself: what in the game tells a player this is on?** Maybe it's the other lad turning his back, or a loose touch.
>
> **If you shout "now!", they'll go on your shout, not on what they saw.** That's fine if you just want them to get the pressing right. But it won't show you they can spot the moment themselves.
>
> **Pointing them at the clue is different from giving the answer.** "Watch when he turns his back" leaves the spotting to them.
>
> **Don't worry about telling them things.** Showing, explaining and calling are all fine. Just know which part you've done for them.

## 39. Simplicity gate

**Research-level distinctions:** ecological information, specifying variables, contextual priors, occlusion, augmented feedback, KR / KP, guidance, attentional focus, substitution, before / during / after.

**Smallest useful distinction for a grassroots coach:**
- **"Is the game telling them, or am I?"** (game information vs coach-supplied), and
- **"Am I pointing them to the clue, or giving them the answer?"** (highlight vs supply).
- Both sit **under** the frozen question: *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"*
- **No new coach-facing question is frozen here.** "What in the game tells a player this is on, and is that still in my practice?" is carried as an **internal design check**.

**Terms that must not reach coaches:** specifying information, affordance, occlusion, KR / KP, guidance hypothesis, substitution, priors.

## 40. Taxonomy verdict

### **C — INFORMATION splits into GAME INFORMATION and COACH-SUPPLIED INFORMATION** (with feedback flagged to separate further).

1. **GAME INFORMATION: a property of the problem.**
   - What the practice makes available to perceive: opponents' and teammates' movement, spacing, ball flight, targets, score / time, player talk.
   - **Mostly produced by other levers** (SPACE, NUMBERS, DIRECTION, GAME MOMENT). Changing those changes the information.
   - **Residue** the other levers do not describe: **deliberately changing what can be perceived** (occlusion, masking, removing a source such as the bowler, making moments announce themselves or not, silent vs talking games as an effect, predictability). Candidate label: **INFORMATION AVAILABLE** (provisional).
   - Whether this residue is large enough to stand as a CHANGE THE PROBLEM property, or should sit as a note on each lever: **for the final gate**.
2. **COACH-SUPPLIED INFORMATION: a separate family (help, not problem).**
   - Instructions, cues, calls, demonstrations, diagrams / video, priors, questions.
   - Changes **how much of the solution players receive**, which DECISION ALLOCATION reads, and can **substitute** for game information (IN-2).
   - **Timing** (before / during / after) is a required descriptor.
3. **FEEDBACK: flagged to split.** Information *about* an action, after or during it. A coaching **process**, not a practice-design property.
4. **Attentional focus, self-control, questioning style:** coaching-intervention material for later work, not practice design.

**Not A:** one lever cannot hold both the problem and the help.
**Not B alone:** the game-information side belongs to the problem.
**Not D:** DECISION ALLOCATION already is the lens; INFORMATION is content it analyses.
**Not E:** the coach-supplied side has no other home.
**F partly:** feedback may need its own home. **G:** not unresolved; the split is clear, its boundaries are for the gate.

## 41. Architecture implication

**What does INFORMATION add that the other levers and Decision Allocation don't explain?**
- **Coach-supplied information** (instructions, calls, demonstrations, questions, feedback) is **not described by any lever**. DECISION ALLOCATION analyses *what it decides*, but is not the thing itself.
- **Substitution** (coach signal replacing game information) is not visible from SPACE / NUMBERS / TIME / DIRECTION / SCORING / ACTIONS / GAME MOMENT.
- **Deliberate changes to what can be perceived** (occlusion, removing an information source) are a small residue on the problem side.

**Is changing information changing the problem, or changing how much help players get?**
- **Both, and that is exactly why INFORMATION splits.**
  - Changing **game information** changes **the problem** (IN-1).
  - Adding **coach information** changes **how much help** players get (DA-1), and in the case of substitution it **also changes the problem** by removing the recognition part.

**Architecture tested (provisional, not frozen):**

```
CHOOSE THE PROBLEM
  GAME MOMENT (incl. score / time scenarios; starting orientation)

CHANGE THE PROBLEM
  SPACE · NUMBERS · TIME · DIRECTION · SCORING · ACTIONS
  + candidate: INFORMATION AVAILABLE (what the practice lets players perceive)  ← gate decides

SUPPLY / SHAPE (what the coach adds)
  COACH-SUPPLIED INFORMATION: instructions, cues, calls, demonstrations, diagrams, priors, questions
  (timing: before / during)
  flagged: FEEDBACK (after / about the action) → coaching process

CROSS-FRAMEWORK
  DECISION ALLOCATION (reads what supplied information decides)
  CF-1 v2 (unchanged; applies to calls and cues)

REFERENCE
  Official game / rules
```

**No symmetry forced.** The problem-side residue may prove too small to list; the supply family may later include non-informational coaching acts.
