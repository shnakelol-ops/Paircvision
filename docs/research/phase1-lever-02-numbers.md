# PáircVision Phase 1 — Practice Design Lever #2: NUMBERS

**Type:** Evidence review. Research only: no software, drills, sessions or UI.
**Companion documents:** [Lever 01 SPACE review](./phase1-lever-01-space.md) · [Lever 01 SPACE final gate](./phase1-lever-01-space-final-gate.md)
**Status:** v1, September 2026

> **Carried forward from SPACE.** Constraints change the *probabilities and opportunities* players experience. A behaviour **produced** inside a constrained game is not proof it has been **learned**. This document uses "produced / appeared" for what happens during a practice game. It uses "learned" only where retention or transfer was actually measured.
>
> **Decision vocabulary (never conflated):** **more decisions** (frequency) ≠ **faster decisions** (time) ≠ **better decisions** (quality / appropriateness) ≠ **learned decisions** (lasting change, shown in retention or transfer).

## 0. Method, labels and access limits

### 0.1 Evidence labels (required on every important claim)

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **RESEARCH EVIDENCE** | Peer-reviewed empirical study or review. The strength is stated separately. |
| **ESTABLISHED PRACTICE** | Widely used, often governing-body-endorsed. Not tested for the claimed learning effect. |
| **PRACTITIONER OPINION** | Coaching resources, commentary, industry analytics. |
| **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS** | Our own inference. Untested. |

Confidence levels are **High / Moderate / Low / Very low**, defined as in Lever 01.

### 0.2 Access limitations (stated explicitly)

- The research environment's network proxy **blocked full-text access** to PubMed/PMC, Frontiers, ResearchGate, SAGE, Taylor & Francis, JSCR/Ovid and most university repositories.
- Every study below was verified through **search-engine indexes of publisher pages, abstracts and repository metadata**. **Everything in this document is abstract-level verification unless stated otherwise.**
- Items where even the abstract-level detail is thin, or the author list could not be confirmed, are marked **†**.
- Variable-level results (exact effect sizes, directions for secondary variables) should be confirmed from full text before PáircVision surfaces any statement to coaches.

### 0.3 Honest summary of the evidence base

- **Most NUMBERS research is soccer, acute (single session) and about physical load.** Tactical positional-data studies are growing. Studies of technical counts are common.
- **Learning studies are rare.** We found **one** longitudinal study that manipulated numerical superiority as its main variable and measured decision-making change: Práxedes et al. (2018). Its design has important limits (§5).
- A 2021 systematic review of rugby SSGs found **no studies of tactical behaviour** in rugby. Almost all rugby SSG research is physical (Zanin et al., 2021).
- **Gaelic football:** no peer-reviewed study of player-number manipulation was found. **Hurling:** one physical-load study on 4-, 5- and 6-a-side formats.
- **The key learning question has not been answered.** No study shows that repeated practice in artificial numerical superiority improves players' ability to **recognise and exploit naturally occurring overloads once the artificial overload is removed**. This is stated plainly in §5.

## 1. Theoretical frame: what "numbers" changes

1. **Numbers change the information available, not just the headcount.** Each added teammate adds passing options (affordances); each added opponent removes or threatens them. What players perceive is *local*: who is near the ball, who can close, who is free (RESEARCH EVIDENCE: Passos et al., 2008; Vilar et al., 2013).
2. **Numbers and space are coupled.** With fixed dimensions, changing numbers changes area per player (ApP). With constant ApP, changing numbers still changes the number of options, the density of interactions and each player's share of the ball. **NUMBERS cannot be analysed independently of SPACE** (§3).
3. **Local vs global numbers.** A 7v7 game contains constantly shifting local 2v1s, 3v2s and 1v2s. Matches are decided in these local relations (RESEARCH EVIDENCE: Vilar, Araújo, Davids & Bar-Yam, 2013). Global manipulations such as 6v4 change how often and how easily those local relations occur. They do not *create* the skill of recognising them.
4. **Degeneracy.** Teams can keep similar team-to-team relations while changing how individuals coordinate. Silva et al. (2016) found that in 3v3, 4v4 and 5v5 youth games, player dispersion increased but team "separateness" stayed the same (RESEARCH EVIDENCE). The same team-level picture can hide different individual behaviour.

## 2. Manipulation-by-manipulation evidence

Format: **MANIPULATION → OBSERVED/EXPECTED EFFECT → MECHANISM → EVIDENCE → CONTEXT → POSSIBLE SIDE EFFECT → CONFIDENCE**

### 2.1 Equal numbers: 1v1, 2v2, 3v3, 4v4 … larger-sided games

**Observed effects: fewer players per side**
- ↑ heart rate, blood lactate and RPE (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**; High for soccer).
  - Hill-Haas et al. (2009): 2v2 > 4v4 and 6v6 for lactate, HR and RPE, with **ApP held constant**, which shows a numbers effect independent of area.
  - Katis & Kellis (2009): 3v3 > 6v6.
  - Klusemann et al. (2012), basketball: 2v2 > 4v4.
- ↑ technical actions per player (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**; High across sports).
  - Soccer: Katis & Kellis (2009); Owen et al. (2011), 3v3 vs 9v9 in elite professionals; Jones & Drust (2007), about three times the ball contacts in 4v4 vs 8v8 in elite youth.
  - Basketball: 2v2 increased dribbles, passes, shots, rebounds and ball screens per player vs 4v4 (Klusemann et al., 2012).
  - Field hockey: fewer players increased technical actions per player (Timmerman et al., 2019).
  - Youth soccer competition: 5v5 > 7v7 in ball contacts and involvement at U9 (German J Exerc Sport Res, 2021). In 4v4 vs 7v7, non-dominant children increased their actions more (+143%) than dominant children (+72%) (PLoS ONE, 2021, "How to improve technical and tactical actions of dominant and non-dominant players in children's football?" †).
  - Youth ice hockey: roughly 2× puck touches and 6× shots in small-area games (**PRACTITIONER OPINION**: federation / NHL analytics).
- With **fixed dimensions**, fewer players → higher external running load, because ApP is larger (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**: rugby SSG literature summarised in Zanin et al., 2021; hurling 4-a-side had the highest relative intensity, Malone et al., JSCR, doi …1628 †).

**Mechanism.** Fewer players means each player is closer to the ball more often, has fewer teammates to "hide" behind, and must cover more roles. At constant ApP, fewer players also means shorter distances between everyone and more duels per minute.

**Side effects**
- Physiological intensity rises, and higher intensity has been associated with lower decision quality (2025 SPACE-gate study, *J Sports Sci Med*).
- Very small formats remove the team-organisation problems (depth, cover, balance, switching) present in the full game.
- 1v1 and 2v2 may teach duelling but not team shape (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Confidence.** Physical load and involvement: **High**. Tactical representativeness of very small formats: **Low**. Learning: **Very low**.

### 2.2 Attacking overloads (2v1, 3v2, 4v3, 5v4, 6v4 …)

**Observed effects for the team in superiority (acute)**
- ↓ physiological intensity and high-intensity running, ↑ walking (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate: synthesised in a 2026 *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* review, doi:10.3389/fspor.2026.1813770 †; Nunes et al., 2020, 4v2–4v6 possession games; Sampaio et al., 2014).
- ↑ passes and total actions (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate; same review).
- ↑ team length and width, more occupation of space near the opponent's goal, more attacking opportunities (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate).
- Adding teammates ↑ longitudinal movement synchronisation and ↓ distance to the nearest teammate. This comes from U18 11v12, 11v13 and 11v14 games (PLoS ONE, 2022, "Amplifying the effects of adding extra players…") (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- **Basketball:** 4v3 half-court games increased group tactical-technical behaviour and lowered physical responses compared with 3v3 (Bredt et al., 2022, *IJSSC* 17(5):1079–1088 †, detail abstract-level).

**Observed effects for the team in inferiority (acute)**
- More compact, closer together, protecting the goal (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate).
  - Silva et al. (2014), 5v5 / 5v4 / 5v3: underloaded teams' compactness and relative position changed, moderated by skill level.
  - Gonçalves et al. (2016), 4v3, 4v5, 4v7.
  - The 2022 PLoS ONE 11v12–14 study: high inferiority → more compact, more synchronised.
- **High inferiority can reduce physical output and exploratory movement** (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate; 2026 review).

**Exploitation vs opportunity.** Superiority increases *opportunities* to progress. It does not guarantee exploitation even in real matches:
- NHL 5v4 power plays convert around 20–25% (**PRACTITIONER OPINION** / league statistics).
- Handball 7v6 empty-goal attacks were about 42–53% effective at recent championships, with a notable risk of conceding into the empty goal (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, observational: Frontiers in Sports and Active Living, 2024, PMC10973547; IJPAS, 2024).

**Mechanism.** The extra attacker cannot be marked. Defenders must choose between pressing the ball and covering space, and one option is usually free. As superiority grows, the defenders' choice becomes easier to punish and needs less attacking skill.

**Side effects (see §2.14 for large vs small)**
- With a large overload, possession becomes too easy. Attackers may never need to *create* an advantage, disguise intention or play under pressure (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**; consistent with the load and exploration findings).
- The defending team gets repeated failure, may become passive, and may learn a deep-block response that doesn't fit balanced play (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Confidence.** Acute load and positional effects: **Moderate**. Learning: **Low** (only Práxedes et al., 2018; see §5).

### 2.3 Defensive overloads / attacking underloads

**Expected effect.** Attackers face more pressure. Space and time shrink. Attackers must play faster, dribble or retain the ball against more pressers, or play direct. Defenders get more successful presses.

**Evidence**
- Symmetrical to §2.2: the inferior (attacking) team shows more compactness and, at high inferiority, less exploration (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- The 2022 PLoS ONE study suggests high inferiority "amplifies perceptual demands and decreases available space and time" and may prepare players to face stronger opponents (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE** for the acute effect; the preparation claim is the **authors' interpretation**).
- AFL 5v6 produced more technical events under greater physical and time pressure than 5v5, 6v6 or 7v7 in sub-elite youth (Bonney, Ball, Berry & Larkin, 2020, *J Sports Sci* 38(21):2430–2436) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).

**Side effects.** Technical breakdown, low success rates, frustration, and learned "hopeful long ball" solutions (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Confidence.** **Low–Moderate** acute. **Very low** for learning.

### 2.4 Neutral players / floaters / jokers

**Observed effects**
- **Floaters work less hard.** Physical load increases from external floaters, to internal floaters, to regular players (IJSPP 2025, "External load between floater types and regular players…"; Rodríguez-Fernández et al., 2025) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate). Floaters are used for return-to-play load management (**ESTABLISHED PRACTICE**).
- **Outside floaters can reduce penetration.** In 54 Brazilian elite-academy players, outside floaters produced significant decreases in the tactical principles of **penetration (−40%), offensive coverage (−22%) and concentration (−25%)**, and an increase in defensive unity (+14%) (FUT-SAT assessment; "Effect of outside floaters on soccer players' tactical behaviour in SSCGs", 2021 †) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- **Floater rules change team shape, and differently by age.** In U13 and U14 players, floater formats increased team width, and the two-floater format reduced length (Praça et al., 2022, *IJSSC*) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- **Floater position matters.** Central vs wide vs inner-zone floater positions changed performance in 6v6 + 2 floaters (Paul, Mathes, Maurus & Memmert, 2025, *IJSSC* †) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low; direction to confirm).
- **Outer floaters and scoring targets** change team dispersion and shape (Castellano, Silva, Usabiaga & Barreira, 2016, *J Hum Kinet*) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low).
- **Inside floater** effects on tactical behaviour were studied by Moniz et al. (2020), *J Hum Kinet* 71:167–177 (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**; detail †).

**Mechanism.** A floater is a guaranteed free option who belongs to whoever has the ball. The opposition cannot press them effectively (especially outside floaters, who are often touch-limited and unopposed).

**Side effects**
- **An artificial escape route.** When pressed, players can recycle to a player who in real games would be marked or would not exist. The drop in penetration and offensive coverage with outside floaters is consistent with this (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE** for the acute drop; the "escape route learning" is a **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).
- The floater's own load and involvement are unrepresentative.
- Defenders learn to ignore the floater, which is a habit that transfers badly if an opponent in that position is real (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Confidence.** "Floaters increase possession-related actions and reduce load for the floater": **Moderate**. "Outside floaters reduce penetration": **Low–Moderate** (one study). Learning: **Very low**.

### 2.5 Inside vs outside neutrals

| | Inside (infield) floater | Outside (perimeter/end) floater |
|---|---|---|
| Physical load | Higher than outside, lower than regular players (RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate) | Lowest (RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate) |
| Pressure on floater | Can be pressed | Usually unpressable (often touch-limited) |
| Tactical effect | Changes internal support structure (Moniz et al., 2020 †) | ↓ penetration, offensive coverage and concentration (2021 study †) |
| Representativeness | Closer to a real teammate | Closer to a "wall" or safe recycle; less like any real player (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS) |
| Confidence | Low | Low–Moderate |

### 2.6 Permanent vs temporary overloads

- **Temporary (switching every minute) vs balanced.** Canton, Torrents, Ric, Gonçalves, Sampaio & Hristovski (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:1968: GK+4v4+GK balanced vs imbalances that changed every minute, in U15 and U23 players. **Long-term exploratory breadth increased in the U23 group only** (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
  - Age moderated the effect: younger players did not show the increase.
  - This is acute, within-session exploration, not learning.
- **Permanent overloads:** see §2.2. The acute effects are lower load and more passing for the superior team, and compaction for the inferior team.
- **Expected difference (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS, theory-consistent).** Temporary overloads force players to *detect* when the overload exists. Permanent overloads remove the need to detect it. This is the crux of the recognition question and **has not been tested for learning**.

**Confidence.** **Low** (acute). **Very low** (learning).

### 2.7 Transition-created overloads

**Expected effect.** After a turnover, overloads arise naturally because the defence is disorganised. Practising transitions exposes players to *genuine*, time-limited overloads.

**Evidence**
- **Match evidence.** Counter-attacks against an imbalanced defence were more effective than elaborate attacks in Norwegian professional soccer (Tenga, Holme, Ronglan & Bahr, 2010, *J Sports Sci* 28(3):237–244) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate).
- **AFL tracking (2019 season).** Greater team numerical advantage increased the likelihood of winning clearance possession and of scoring from inside-50 entries (PLoS ONE, 2021, "Team numerical advantage in Australian rules football…") (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate; highly relevant to Gaelic football).
- **Training.** In U17 soccer, Rochael & Praça (2024, *IJSSC*) compared GK+4v4+GK and GK+5v4+GK counter-attack SSGs, with and without time pressure. Tactical decision-making and execution were **better without time pressure** (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate; acute). A companion study found that time pressure and superiority changed interpersonal coordination (IJPAS, 2024).

**Side effect.** Coaches often *add* time pressure to make transitions realistic. The one study found time pressure lowered decision and execution quality acutely. Whether it helps learning is unknown.

**Confidence.** Match relevance of transition overloads: **Moderate**. Training effects: **Low**.

### 2.8 Recovering / chasing defenders

**Expected effect.** Attackers must exploit the overload *before* it disappears. That links recognition to time, which is representative of real transitions.

**Evidence.** **No peer-reviewed training study was found** that isolates recovering or chasing defenders in invasion games. It is widely used in rugby and soccer coaching resources (**ESTABLISHED PRACTICE / PRACTITIONER OPINION**).

**Mechanism (theory).** The overload has a closing time window, so the information that specifies "overload now" is dynamic, as in matches (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Confidence.** **Very low.**

### 2.9 Delayed player entry

Same status as §2.8. **No isolated research found. ESTABLISHED PRACTICE only.**

It is theoretically closer to match reality than permanent overloads because the numerical relation *changes within a single possession* (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**). Canton et al. (2019) is the nearest evidence: changing imbalances increased exploration in adults only.

**Confidence: Very low.**

### 2.10 Local overloads within an otherwise balanced game

**Observed effects (match evidence)**
- Local numerical dominance near the ball is linked to goal-scoring opportunities (Vilar et al., 2013) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- In the AFL, team numerical advantage mattered for scoring. **But more total players inside 50 was associated with a lower probability of scoring, regardless of numerical advantage** (PLoS ONE, 2021) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate). Congestion can cancel a numerical edge.
- AFL teams were largely **outnumbered** when the ball was in their forward 50 and attained numerical advantage in their defensive 50 (same study). Natural overloads are zone-dependent.

**Training.** No study isolates a zone-based local overload inside a balanced game for learning. Silva et al. (2015; see §3) is the closest.

**Confidence.** Match importance: **Moderate**. Training design effects: **Very low**.

### 2.11 Numerical superiority around the ball vs away from it

**Evidence.** Local superiority *near the ball* is what matches reward (Vilar et al., 2013). AFL advantage effects were zone- and phase-specific (PLoS ONE, 2021). **No training study compares on-ball vs off-ball overloads.**

**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS.** An overload far from the ball produces nothing unless players can switch play to it fast enough. That makes it a test of switching and scanning, not of 2v1 exploitation. An overload near the ball tests immediate exploitation.

**Confidence.** **Very low** for training.

### 2.12 Changing team numbers while pitch area stays fixed

**Observed effects**
- Fewer players → larger ApP → higher running load and, often, higher intensity (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate–High: Castellano et al., 2016, *PLoS ONE*; rugby SSG research; Bonney et al., 2020).
- AFL 5v5, 5v6, 6v6 and 7v7 on a fixed field changed both technical and physical outcomes, with 5v6 giving the most pressured technical events (Bonney et al., 2020).

**Confound.** This changes *both* numbers and density, so effects cannot be attributed to numbers alone (see §3).

**Confidence.** **Moderate.**

### 2.13 Changing numbers while ApP is held approximately constant

**Observed effects**
- **Intensity rises with fewer players even at constant ApP** (Hill-Haas et al., 2009) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate).
- The same ApP with different numbers does **not** produce equivalent demands, and age moderates this (Castellano et al., 2016, *PLoS ONE*: 7, 9 and 11-a-side × 100, 200 and 300 m² per player) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Moderate).
- In 3v3, 4v4 and 5v5, player dispersion increased but team separateness was unchanged (Silva et al., 2016) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).

**Implication.** Numbers have effects of their own beyond density: more options, more roles, a shared ball.

**Confidence.** **Moderate.**

### 2.14 Large vs small numerical differences

| Difference | Superior team (acute) | Inferior team (acute) | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small (+1, e.g., 4v3, 5v4, 11v12) | Modest ↓ load; modest ↑ passes and width | Moderate compaction | RESEARCH EVIDENCE (Nunes et al., 2020; PLoS ONE 2022; Silva et al., 2014) | Moderate |
| Moderate (+2) | Further ↓ load; ↑ expansion | More compact; some ↓ exploration | Same | Low–Moderate |
| Large (+3 or more, e.g., 4v7, 11v14, 6v3) | Low load; possession very easy | Very compact; ↓ physical output and exploration; perceptual overload | RESEARCH EVIDENCE (Gonçalves et al., 2016; PLoS ONE 2022; 2026 review †) | Low–Moderate |

**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS.** Small differences keep the problem hard enough that players must *find* the free player. Large differences make the free player obvious and remove the need to create or disguise the advantage. **Untested for learning.**

### 2.15 Goalkeeper involvement as an additional player

**Observed effects**
- Goalkeeper presence changes outfield load and tactics, but **the direction of HR effects is inconsistent**. Some studies found lower HR with goalkeepers on small 5v5 pitches; others found higher HR reserve with goalkeepers in 8v8 (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low; mixed).
- Wildcard (floater) type and goalkeeper presence interact (Rodríguez-Fernández et al., 2025) (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, Low–Moderate).
- **Handball 7v6 (goalkeeper replaced by a court player).** Effectiveness is about 42–53%. Teams concede a considerable share of direct goal-to-goal shots (≈16% of sequences, ≈57% effective). The option is used more to restore equality after sanctions than to create superiority (**RESEARCH EVIDENCE**, observational).
  - Lesson: **a real "extra player" overload carries a real counter-risk**. Practice overloads that remove that risk are less representative (**PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**).

**Gaelic and hurling relevance (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS).**
- Goalkeepers are central to kickout and puckout restarts. A goalkeeper joining outfield play (in build-up, or as a "sweeper") creates overloads with the same counter-risk as handball's empty goal.
- The 2025 GAA football rules (players required to stay in each half) constrain *how* overloads can be created in open play. That makes numbers in Gaelic football partly structural.
- No study exists.

**Confidence.** **Low.**

## 3. SPACE × NUMBERS interaction (mandatory)

**Core finding.** Space and numbers **interact**. They are not independent levers. The same ratio, or the same ApP, can produce different behaviour depending on how it was reached.

### 3.1 Evidence on interaction

| Scenario | What happens | Evidence | Label / confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Numbers change, area constant** | Fewer players → larger ApP → ↑ running load and ↑ involvement per player. More players → congestion, ↓ involvement per player. | Castellano et al. (2016); Bonney et al. (2020); rugby SSG literature (Zanin et al., 2021) | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate |
| **Area changes, ratio constant** (e.g., 4v4 small vs large) | Larger area ↑ load, spread, passing success; smaller area ↑ duels and turnovers | Lever 01 (Clemente et al., 2023 meta-analysis) | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, High (load) |
| **ApP constant, total numbers change** | Not equivalent. Fewer players → higher intensity (Hill-Haas et al., 2009); demands differ by format and age (Castellano et al., 2016); dispersion changes while team separateness stays stable (Silva et al., 2016) | As cited | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate |
| **Reaching a given ApP via numbers vs via dimensions** | **Changing numbers gave more free space around each player. Changing field dimensions gave more advantageous local numerical relations and broader individual spatial distributions.** | Silva, Esteves, Correia, Davids, Araújo & Garganta (2015), *IJPAS* 15(2):641–659; U15, ApP 118 / 133 / 152 m² | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Low–Moderate. **Key interaction evidence.** |
| **Local overload changes effective space without cones moving** | Superior teams expand (length and width) and play closer to the opponent's goal; inferior teams compress. Effective playing space changes with no change to the pitch. | Silva et al. (2014); Gonçalves et al. (2016); PLoS ONE (2022); 2026 review † | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate |
| **Same superiority in tight vs large space** | Area and numerical unbalance interact on external load and passing actions in 4v3, 4v4 and 4v5 across 20×15, 25×20 and 30×25 m | Nunes, Gonçalves, Coutinho, Nakamura & Travassos (2021), *IJSSC* (detail †) | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Low |
| **More bodies in the scoring zone** | More total players inside 50 ↓ scoring probability, *regardless* of numerical advantage | AFL tracking (PLoS ONE, 2021) | RESEARCH EVIDENCE, Moderate. **Match-level interaction.** |

### 3.2 SPACE × NUMBERS interaction matrix (reasoning aid)

| | **Tight space** | **Large space** |
|---|---|---|
| **Equal numbers, few players** (2v2–4v4) | Max duels, turnovers, HR; high technical breakdown risk; high decision *frequency* | High running load; many involvements; transitions long |
| **Equal numbers, many players** (7v7+) | Congestion; low involvement per player; possession hard | Most match-like team shape (if ApP is match-proportioned); lower involvement per player |
| **Small overload (+1)** | Superiority still hard to use; must find the free player under pressure (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS: most "recognition-demanding") | Superiority easy to use; inferior team stretched; possession likely |
| **Large overload (+3 or more)** | Inferior team packs a small area; superior team still needs combination play | Possession trivial; inferior team passive / chasing; least representative (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS) |
| **Floaters** | Floater becomes the main escape valve | Floater adds width; penetration may fall (outside floaters) |

*Confidence for the whole matrix: cells combine Moderate evidence on each lever with **Low** evidence on their interaction. Treat it as a hypothesis grid, not a prescription.*

## 5. Learning and transfer

### 5.1 What exists

| Study | Design | Numbers manipulation | Outcome | What it shows | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 5.2 The central question: does repeated superiority improve recognition of natural overloads?

**This has not been demonstrated.** No study found in any invasion sport trains with artificial numerical superiority, removes it, and then tests whether players better *recognise* and *exploit* naturally occurring overloads in balanced games or competition. Práxedes et al. (2018) comes closest, but measured pass decisions in modified games, not the detection of spontaneous overloads.

### 5.3 How it could be tested (method, not software)

The same principles as the Lever 01 gate apply:
1. A stable **balanced reference game**.
2. A **baseline**.
3. An intervention in which the overload is permanent vs temporary vs transition-created.
4. **Delayed** retention (at least a week).
5. **Detection-conditional measures.** When a local overload naturally occurs in the reference game, how often and how quickly does the team exploit it? Compare that with how often it plays into equal or inferior local situations.
6. A comparison group.

The same measurement could be applied to competition, *if* local numerical relations can be coded from video.

## 9. Common coaching assumptions: what the evidence does and doesn't support

| Assumption | Verdict | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **"Overloads teach support."** | **Produces support-like behaviour; learning not demonstrated.** | Superiority acutely increases passing options and team spread and reduces distance to the nearest teammate (RESEARCH EVIDENCE). Only Práxedes et al. (2018) shows improved *pass decisions* over time, in average-skill U12s. No study shows players learn to *create* support in balanced play. |
| **"3v2 teaches players to exploit an extra player."** | **Not demonstrated.** | No study tests recognition and exploitation of natural overloads after training with artificial ones. Even genuine match overloads convert modestly (power plays ≈20–25%; handball 7v6 ≈42–53%). |
| **"Underloads improve defending."** | **Produces compactness; learning not demonstrated; possible negative side effects.** | Inferior teams compact and synchronise (RESEARCH EVIDENCE). High inferiority can ↓ physical output and exploration. No evidence of better defending in balanced play afterwards. |
| **"Floaters improve possession."** | **Partially supported acutely; with a cost.** | Floaters create constant superiority and ↑ passes. Outside floaters ↓ penetration, offensive coverage and concentration (−22% to −40%). Floaters have lower load. No learning evidence. |
| **"Small-sided games increase involvement."** | **Supported (High).** | Consistent across soccer, basketball, hockey and youth formats, especially for less dominant children. Involvement is not learning. |
| **"Fewer players means more touches and decisions."** | **Touches: supported (High). Decision *opportunities*: supported. Faster / better / learned decisions: not shown.** | Per-player actions rise. Decision quality was not measured in most studies, and higher intensity may lower quality. |
| **"Numerical superiority creates more space."** | **Partially supported; context-dependent.** | Superior teams expand and there is more free space around players when numbers are manipulated (Silva et al., 2015). But in the AFL, more bodies in the scoring zone ↓ scoring despite advantage. Superiority into a congested area can *reduce* useful space. |
| **"Numerical inferiority teaches players to work harder."** | **Not supported; partly contradicted.** | Inferior teams often *reduce* physical output and exploratory movement, especially in high inferiority. They compact and protect rather than work harder. No learning evidence. In real matches, numerical inferiority (red card) lowers the penalised team's scoring rate and raises the opponent's (Ridder et al., 1994). |

## 10. Contradictions

1. **Exploration and imbalance.** Temporary imbalances ↑ exploratory breadth in adults (Canton et al., 2019). The 2026 synthesis reports ↓ exploratory behaviour for superior teams and under high inferiority. **Temporary vs permanent** imbalance and **age** may explain the difference. Unresolved.
2. **Superiority and space.** Superiority expands the superior team and creates free space (SSG evidence), but more bodies inside 50 reduced AFL scoring probability despite advantage (match evidence).
3. **Floaters.** They ↑ possession-type actions but ↓ penetration. What they "improve" depends on the target.
4. **Goalkeeper presence and HR.** Opposite directions across studies.
5. **Superiority helps decisions (Práxedes) vs time pressure hurts decisions (Rochael & Praça).** Both support "more time → better decisions *during* practice". Neither shows what transfers to pressured, balanced competition.
6. **Numbers vs dimensions.** Reaching the same ApP by changing numbers gave more free space per player. Changing dimensions gave more favourable local numerical relations (Silva et al., 2015). **Same ApP, different games.**
7. **Práxedes: superiority improved, equality didn't.** This could mean superiority is more effective. It could equally be an order, ceiling or timing effect of the non-counterbalanced design.

## 11. Ten major unanswered questions

1. **Does repeated artificial superiority improve recognition and exploitation of naturally occurring overloads once it is removed?** Untested.
2. **Temporary vs permanent overloads:** which produces better *detection* of when an overload exists, measured in balanced reference games and competition?
3. **What size of overload is optimal** for learning (not just acute behaviour) at different ages and skill levels?
4. **Do outside floaters create an "escape route" habit** that reduces penetration in balanced games afterwards?
5. **Do permanent underloads teach defenders useful compactness, or unrealistic passivity** that fails in balanced play?
6. **How do SPACE and NUMBERS interact for learning?** Is a +1 overload in a tight space a better "recognition" task than in a large space?
7. **Do recovering-defender and delayed-entry designs**, which create time-limited overloads, transfer better than static overloads?
8. **Gaelic football and hurling:** what natural numerical relations occur around kickouts, puckouts, breaks and turnovers? How do they relate to scoring (an AFL-style analysis)?
9. **How do the 2025 GAA half-restriction rule and sin-bin periods change natural overloads**, and can these serve as natural experiments?
10. **What low-cost, observable indicators** (without tracking data) can show a coach that players are recognising and using local overloads in real matches?

## 12. What PáircVision must NOT claim

1. ❌ "Overloads **teach** support / exploiting the extra player / creating space." They **produce** these opportunities during practice. Learning is not demonstrated.
2. ❌ "Practising 3v2s makes players better at spotting overloads in matches." Untested.
3. ❌ "Underloads improve defending" or "make players work harder." Inferiority often *reduces* output and exploration.
4. ❌ "Floaters improve possession" without stating that outside floaters reduced penetration in the one tactical study, and that floater load is unrepresentative.
5. ❌ "Fewer players means better decisions" or "faster decisions." Only more touches and more decision *opportunities* are supported.
6. ❌ "Numerical superiority always creates more space." It can congest the scoring zone (AFL evidence).
7. ❌ That numbers can be understood without space. Same ratio or same ApP ≠ same game.
8. ❌ Any Gaelic football or hurling evidence on tactical or learning effects of numbers. None exists; the hurling study is physical only.
9. ❌ That the black card, or the 2025 GAA rules, "prove" anything about numbers. The data are confounded and unevaluated.
10. ❌ That involvement or enjoyment gains in small formats are the same as long-term skill learning.
11. ❌ Federation and industry figures (e.g., ice hockey touch multipliers, basketball "decisions per session" claims found on coaching websites) presented as research evidence.

## 13. Plain-language summary for coaches (draft; research wording, not UI)

> **NUMBERS: changing who is on each team.**
>
> - **Fewer players:** more touches, more involvement, more running and higher intensity. Quiet players get into the game more. It gives more decisions, not necessarily better ones.
> - **An extra attacker** (e.g., 4v3): the attacking team finds it easier to keep the ball, spreads out and passes more, and usually works less hard. The defending team squeezes together.
> - **A big overload** (e.g., 6v3): possession becomes easy and defenders mostly just protect. It is useful for building confidence, but it may stop attackers needing to *find* the free player.
> - **Floaters:** easy passing, but they can become an escape route. One study found teams went forward much less with outside floaters.
> - **Overloads that come and go** (after a turnover, a late defender, changing every minute) are closer to real matches, where overloads last seconds.
>
> **Numbers and space work together:** the same numbers in a tight or a large area make a different game.
>
> **Most important:** if players use the extra player in your 3v2, that shows the game is working. It doesn't yet show they will spot a real 3v2 in a match. To check, play your normal balanced game, look again next week, and watch matches for moments they had more players near the ball. Did they use them?
