> - Medium; Substack; the Perception & Action Podcast site;
> - independent coaching blogs.
>
> - Every practitioner claim rests on **search-index summaries (SIO)** or abstracts (**AO**).
> - Quotes are **as indexed** and must be checked against the originals.
> - Search summaries sometimes blend a source with the search engine's own gloss. Where that risk is high, the claim is marked **UNR**.
> - The two Kinnerk primary sources (2016 Munster Hurling Academy talk; TGfU.info interview) remain **not accessed**. A third was located (2017 GAA Games Development Conference slides) and **not accessed**.
> - **Conclusions are provisional on access.**

> **Central question.** Why do experienced coaches deliberately remove, require or restrict actions in practice, and how do they judge whether the rule is helping players learn or merely making them comply?
>
> **Second question.** When is deliberately taking a decision away from players good coaching?
>
> **Frozen coach-facing question (Candidate 07):** *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"*
>
> **CF-1 v2:** *behaviour from a supplied condition is not evidence players can make the decisions that condition made for them. Evidence about decisions still available to them is unaffected. This limits inference; it does not mean supplying conditions is poor practice.*
>
> **Standard:** COMPLIANCE ≠ UNDERSTANDING · REPETITION ≠ RECOGNITION · but REMOVING CHOICE ≠ BAD COACHING.

## 0. Labels

- **Statement type:**
  - **DIRECT**: a coach's or federation's own statement, as indexed.
  - **REPORTED**: a journalist's or author's account of what a coach said or does.
  - **INFERENCE**: a reasonable reading of a source.
  - **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION**: our framing, not the practitioner's.
- **Proposition status:** MULTIPLE-PRACTITIONER SUPPORT · SINGLE-PRACTITIONER EXAMPLE · SCIENTIFICALLY CONSISTENT · SCIENTIFICALLY UNTESTED · CONTRADICTED · PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS.
- **Drill websites are not practitioner evidence.** Sites that list games with a one-line rationale are recorded only as evidence of **what is commonly claimed** (folklore), never as coach reasoning.

## 2. Why restrict an action? Stated rationales vs PáircVision interpretation

| Rationale | Stated by a practitioner? | Source | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exaggerate / emphasise a problem** | **YES** (reported and direct) | P8 (Kinnerk, reported), P11 (Pill), P10 (GAA GBA "modification-exaggeration") | The most commonly stated rationale. Pill's example: restricting play in the middle zone "to promote support play and changing the point of attack" |
| **Increase repetitions / exposure** | **YES** | P19 (Upton: increase repetition; challenge point), P1 (FA: the No. 10 receives on the turn more often) | Repetition framed as learning opportunity, not as decision practice |
| **Remove a dominant / habitual solution** | **YES** | P1 (No. 10 "tendency to set back"), P4 ("rein in the tendency of certain players to over-dribble"), P7 (remove the dribble; the ball "can't stick") | See §4 |
| **Force use of a neglected technique** | Partly | P1 (turn on first touch); weak-foot material is mostly commercial (§4) | |
| **Diagnose what the team needs** | **YES** (unexpected) | P7: remove the dribble "and the game tells coaches what their team needs to work on" | A rule used as a **diagnostic probe**, not as teaching |
| **Change others' behaviour (off-ball)** | **YES** | P7: "players who normally stand and watch start cutting" | The rule is on the ball-carrier; the intended change is in **teammates** |
| **Create a team-oriented / tempo "mindset"** | **YES** (claim) | P4 ("team-oriented, quick-passing mindset") | Mindset claim = PRACTITIONER BELIEF; untested |
| **Simplify the problem** | Partly | P11 ("reduction … of player decisions") | Pill names reduction/elimination of decisions **as a legitimate design tool** |
| **Coordinate team behaviour** | Not for rules specifically | Candidate 07 (responsibilities at restarts) | Coordination was achieved by **allocation**, not prohibition, in the sources found |
| **Safety** | Governing-body level only | GAA Go Games; rugby contact-modified formats | Not a learning rationale (scientific audit §20) |
| **Physical demand** | Drill level only | Pressing games with a 5–8 s regain rule (e.g., FIFA Training Centre "fast recapture") | Rationale in drill material: physical and psychological resilience. Low weight |
| **Create deliberate failure** | **YES** (pedagogy) | P20 (Renshaw & Chow) | "Practitioners may sometimes manipulate constraints to deliberately create such an outcome"; high risk, needs support |
| **Individualise within a team game** | **YES** | P1 ("tasking or constraining players individually … to practise something unique to them within the context of the game") | A rule applied to **one** player |

**Finding.**
- Practitioners state more rationales than "teach a decision". **Exposure, repetition, habit-breaking, diagnosis, individualisation** and **exaggeration** all appear.
- The rationale "**teach players when to choose X**" is rarely stated for a restriction. Where it is implied (P4 "mindset"; drill-site "think faster"), it is **belief, not evidence**.
- **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION:** most stated rationales are about **what players meet and do** (exposure, reps), not about **what players choose**. That is consistent with CF-1 v2: practitioners mostly do not claim the decision was learned.

## 3. Two-touch / touch limits (priority)

| Position | Who | What they say (as indexed) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **For** | P4 (TheMastermindSite) | Touch limits are "a standard method of **removing the option to dribble**"; good coaches "rein in the tendency of certain players to over-dribble, and instill a team-oriented, quick-passing mindset" | PRACTITIONER BELIEF (blog) |
| **For (technique)** | P6 (Cruyff) | The rondo teaches, among much else, "how to play **one touch** soccer". Elsewhere: technique is "passing the ball with one touch, with the right speed, at the correct foot of your teammate" | DIRECT (quoted). A **technical** rationale, not a thinking-speed claim |
| **Against (over-use)** | P2 (citing a Mönchengladbach first-team assistant) | Limiting touches is "occasionally counter-productive": "**if I'm never allowed to dribble, how am I supposed to know how to do it?**" Restricting an outstanding dribbler "will only diminish what they are very good at" | REPORTED (coach name **UNR**) |
| **Against (decision)** | P2, P3 | Limiting touches **forces** decisions rather than **testing or improving** them; kids need to learn when to dribble, pass or shoot, and "limiting touches does not test that awareness" | DIRECT (blogs) |
| **Against (developmental origin)** | P2 | Unrealistic constraints such as limited touches came into youth football from the **professional game** and outcome-focused training | PRACTITIONER OPINION |
| **Alternative** | P5 (Tristan Thomas) | Instead of "two-touch max", award extra goals if the finish, assist and pre-assist are two touches or fewer. That "would still encourage quick combinations in the final third, however **wouldn't make players force two touches if it wasn't the correct moment**" | DIRECT. Converts a **rule** into a **reward** (SCORING) to keep the choice alive |
| **Federation caution** | FA-adjacent summaries (SIO) | "Over-constraining tasks – such as rigid touch limits – may reduce decision authenticity by simplifying players' action possibilities" | **UNR**: may be the search engine's gloss rather than FA wording |

### "Two-touch makes players think faster"

- **Do respected coaches claim it?**
  - **Drill and marketing sites claim it routinely**: "two-touch forces quicker decisions"; "forces players to scan and think ahead". Recorded as **COACHING FOLKLORE**.
  - **Cruyff's** quoted rationale is **technical** (one-touch passing quality), not cognitive speed.
  - **No elite coach primary statement** was found claiming touch limits make players *think* faster.
- **Practitioners who question it** (P2, P3) argue the rule **removes** the decision it is supposed to train.
- **Status:** PRACTITIONER BELIEF (common in drill media). **Not evidence.** Scientific audit RU-5 stands: touch limits change action structure and load; they are not shown to speed decision-making.

### Other touch-limit questions

| Question | Practitioner answer found |
|---|---|
| When do they avoid touch limits? | For an outstanding dribbler (P2); with young players (P2, P3); when overused (P3) |
| Temporary? | Implied (progressions in rondo material raise or lower the limit by skill level). **No source describes a planned removal** |
| Do players exploit them? | Not described by practitioners. Science shows more **unsuccessful** passes (scientific audit §5) |
| Removed before game play? | Session structures end in a free game (P10, P24). **Not linked to touch limits specifically** |

**Disagreement is real and principled.** Both sides agree on the mechanism: the rule **removes dribbling**. They disagree on whether that removal is the point (P4) or the problem (P2, P3). That is exactly the CF-1 v2 tension, stated in coaches' words.

## 4. Remove the dominant solution

| Example | Source | Stated purpose | Teach the alternative, or expose to it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| No. 10 "no one-touch passes backwards" | P1 (FA) | He "had a tendency to set back when the ball came to his feet, missing opportunities to receive, turn and threaten". The constraint "led to him **receiving on the turn** and turning on his first touch, benefiting … **his techniques and decisions** but also the physical movements he made" | **Both claimed.** The FA claims decision benefit. **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION:** the rule removes the setting-back option, so it creates **exposure** to turning. It does not practise *choosing* between setting back and turning. The decision benefit is an **untested claim** |
| Remove the dribble (basketball) | P7 (Boylan & LaRoche) | "The ball can't stick"; "every catch becomes a decision: **shoot it or move it**"; watchers start cutting; the constraint "doesn't teach any one skill but creates an environment where passing, catching and shot readiness all have to work together" | **Exposure plus a narrowed decision.** Removing one option makes the remaining choices (shoot / pass / cut) more frequent and more urgent. **Explicitly not** claimed to teach when to dribble |
| Limit the over-dribbler | P4 | Rein in over-dribbling | **Suppression**, with a hoped-for mindset change |
| Weak foot only / weak foot counts double | Commercial guides (e.g., youth-training sites) | "Force the weak foot to appear under realistic pressure"; one claims "measurable match transfer within 2 to 3 sessions" | **Exposure.** The transfer claim is **UNSUPPORTED MARKETING**; the scientific audit found technical-test learning only (§19) |
| No kicking / hand-pass only (Gaelic, AFL) | Pill (AFL: kicking game in a larger area, handball game in a smaller one) | Space is changed to suit the allowed skill | **Exposure** to one skill family; no primary rationale found for Gaelic |

**The distinction that matters (PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS, consistent with practitioners):**

| Purpose of banning the easy option | What it can legitimately claim | What it cannot claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Exposure / repetition** of the alternative | More practice of the alternative skill | That players now know *when* to use it |
| **Revealing the rest of the team's problem** (P7) | Teammates must solve support without the escape route | That the banned option was wrong |
| **Diagnosis** (P7) | The coach sees what breaks | Anything about learning |
| **Habit-breaking** (P1, P4) | The habit is interrupted while the rule lasts | That the habit is gone once the rule is removed |

- Practitioners often **blend** these (the FA example claims technique *and* decisions).
- The Mönchengladbach objection (P2) is the mirror image: banning the dominant option can **starve** the player's best weapon.

## 5. Require a behaviour

| Mandate | Practitioner rationale found? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mandatory overlap | **No** (drill libraries only) | |
| Mandatory switch | **No primary rationale.** Closest: SCORING practitioner audit: a bonus rather than a must, "because X is not always right" (P24) | Practitioners who discuss it prefer **reward** over **requirement** |
| Required forward pass / "first pass forward" | **No** | |
| Minimum support / required width | Pill: restricting the middle zone "to promote support play" (a **space** rule, not a mandate) | Implemented through SPACE |
| Mandatory transition response (regain within N seconds) | Drill level (pressing games) | Rationale physical / resilience |
| Must use a target player | **No** | |
| Must offload (rugby) | Drill level: games reward offloads with bonus points; score doesn't count without a pass near the line | Implemented as **SCORING / validity** |

**Stress-test: "If I require the behaviour, have I removed the decision about whether it is appropriate?"**
- **Yes, for that decision** (CF-1 v2). The only practitioner who addresses it directly (P5) says so in plain words and **changes the rule to a reward** for that reason.
- **But it does not follow that the practice is poor.** Legitimate practitioner purposes for requiring an action appear elsewhere:
  - rehearsal of a team pattern (Candidate 07: restart jobs);
  - repetitions of the execution (HOW), timing (WHEN) and coordination still left open (scientific audit §9);
  - deliberate exaggeration (P8, P11).
- **Finding:** practitioners **rarely** explain mandates at all; when they reason about them, they **prefer rewards or space changes** that make the action attractive without making it compulsory. **SINGLE-PRACTITIONER EXAMPLE** (P5) plus the SCORING audit's S3.

## 6. Expose a problem versus prescribe a solution

**Do experienced coaches make an equivalent distinction?**

| Practitioner language | Closest to | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Exaggeration": overstate a **tactical problem** | Problem-amplifying | P8 (Kinnerk, reported: "most top coaches change the rules of a training game to overstate or emphasise a specific tactical problem"); P10; P11 |
| "Exaggeration, reduction **or elimination of player decisions**" | **Both**: Pill names deciding-for-players as one legitimate tool among several | P11 |
| "Changing the constraints **to let them find it**" vs "telling them what to do" | Problem vs solution | P21 (Gray) |
| "Wouldn't make players force two touches if it wasn't the correct moment" | Solution-prescribing rule replaced by an incentive | P5 |
| "Pair freedom with intent: let players solve the problem, but set a clear objective" | Problem framing | Academy marketing (low weight) |
| Remove the dribble "doesn't teach any one skill"; "the game tells coaches what their team needs" | Problem-exposing | P7 |

**Finding.**
- Practitioners **do** make an equivalent distinction, but in their own words: **"exaggerate the problem"** vs **"tell them what to do"**.
- They do **not** use a rule-type binary. The same person (Pill) lists exaggeration *and* elimination of decisions as tools.
- **The binary does not survive as a clean classification** (§23): many rules both raise the frequency of a problem **and** remove part of its solution. The two-touch rule makes support problems more frequent *and* removes dribbling as an answer.
- **Survives as a question of degree**: *how much of the solution does this rule supply?* That is already the DECISION ALLOCATION question.

## 7. Decision allocation in practitioner examples

(WHO / WHAT / WHEN / HOW is a PáircVision lens, not a validated measurement model.)

## 8. Temporary scaffolding

| Question | Finding | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Are artificial rules described as temporary? | **Implied, rarely stated.** Rondo progressions raise or lower touch limits by skill level; GBA sessions move between game and fix | P6-adjacent material; P10 |
| Described as scaffolding? | **Yes, in research on practice**: high-performance Gaelic coaches' practice incorporated "scaffolding principles"; "judicious deviation" from representative play by player need | P14 |
| Described as exaggeration? | **Yes** | P8, P10, P11 |
| Actual progression: rule → behaviour appears → rule reduced → normal game? | **Session-level only**: Play → Freeze → Fix → Play (P10); Play-Practice-Play (P24). **No source describes removing a specific rule and watching whether the behaviour survives** | P10, P24 |
| Do practitioners explicitly check what survives? | **Not found.** One commercial guide advises time for "unrestricted play where players apply learning without constraints … this transfer is where true development occurs" | Commercial (low weight) |

**Comparison with EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN:**
- **EXPOSE:** matches "exaggeration" language (multiple practitioners).
- **EXPLORE:** matches CLA language ("let them find it", P21) but not all practitioners (P4 wants the behaviour, not exploration).
- **RELEASE:** present **structurally** (free game at session end), **not** as a targeted check of a removed rule.
- **RETAIN:** **not found.**
- **Practitioner resemblance does not validate the model.** It remains a **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**.

## 9. "If I took this rule away, would I still want them to choose that?"

Stress-tested against the practitioner purposes found.

| Rule | Answer | What the answer tells the coach | Does the question help? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two-touch | "Sometimes" | The rule forces it even when it is wrong. P5's redesign follows from this answer | **Yes**: this is P5's own reasoning |
| Weak foot only | "No, not always" | The rule is for **exposure**, not choice. Behaviour under it says nothing about choosing the weak foot | **Partly**: the "no" is not a failure; the question could mislead a coach into dropping a legitimate repetition rule |
| Mandatory forward pass | "Only when forward is on" | The rule removes the "is forward on?" decision | Yes |
| Wide-channel requirement | "Only when width helps" | Same | Yes |
| Mandatory overlap | "Only when the overlap is on" | Same | Yes |
| Pass-count rule | "No, I want purposeful passes, not a count" | Warns about purposeless passing (P24) | Yes |
| No-tackle game | "Of course not" | The rule is for **safety / attacker confidence**, not for defenders' decisions | **Fails**: the answer is obvious and irrelevant to the purpose |
| No one-touch backwards (FA No. 10) | "Sometimes setting back is right" | The rule is for **habit-breaking / exposure** | **Partly** |

**Verdict.**
- The question is **useful when the coach's aim is a decision** (tactical rules). It **fails or misleads** when the aim is exposure, technique, habit-breaking or safety.
- It adds **nothing** the frozen question does not already cover: *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"* A coach who answers that honestly already knows the rule is choosing for the players.
- **Genuine gap?** Partly. The frozen question does not prompt the **release** check (take it away and look). But no practitioner used such a question; they used **session structure** (free game at the end) for that job.
- **Recommendation:** **do not add** as a coach-facing question. Carry it to the final gate as a possible **supporting explanation** under the frozen question, not a second question.

## 10. Game authenticity

| Position | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Game forms used individually and contextually at the highest level | P15 | No single "realism" doctrine |
| "Judicious deviation" from representative play for player needs | P14 | High-performance Gaelic; **deliberately less realistic** when needed |

## 18. Technique versus game understanding

- The strongest practitioner-supported restrictions are **technical or habit-based**: one-touch passing quality (P6), receive-and-turn (P1), weak foot (commercial; science: tests only), removing the dribble for catch-and-shoot readiness (P7).
- Claims that a restriction teaches **tactical recognition** (quick-passing "mindset", "think faster", "patience") are **beliefs**, not demonstrated.
- **Rule appropriateness depends on the objective:**
  - If the objective is **doing** (execution, repetitions, a pattern), removing the choice can be exactly right.
  - If the objective is **choosing** (recognising when), removing the choice removes the thing being practised.
- **Caution:** this is **not** "technical = isolated, tactical = game-based". P7's no-dribble game is a live game used partly for technical readiness; P1's constraint sits inside a team game.
- **Status:** PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS, consistent with multiple practitioners and with CF-1 v2.

## 21. The name: ACTION ALLOWANCES

**Practitioner terminology found:**

| Term | Who uses it | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| **Conditions / conditioned games** | UK and Irish coaching (FA, GAA, Upton P19) | Any modification, including space, scoring, numbers |
| **Constraints** | CLA practitioners (P19, P20, P21; FA "constraints") | Everything (task, environment, individual) |
| **Restrictions** | Touch-limit debate (P2, P3, P4, P5) | Prohibitions and limits |
| **Rules** | Everyone, loosely | Everything |
| **Resistances** | Upton (P19), as familiar coaching word | Difficulty-adding conditions |
| **Provocation rules** | Weak-foot guides (low weight) | Rules that provoke a behaviour |
| **Exaggeration / modification** | TGfU, Game Sense, GAA GBA | Design principle, not a category |
| **Action allowances** | **Nobody** | — |

**Findings.**
- Practitioners do **not** separate "rules about actions" from other conditions. The distinction is **internal to PáircVision**.
- "Action allowances" is unfamiliar and slightly bureaucratic. "Allowances" also reads as **permissions only**, while the category includes **requirements**.
- "Restrictions" misses **requirements** (must overlap).
- "Conditions" and "constraints" are **too broad**: they cover all levers.

**Options for the internal term:**

| Candidate | Clarity | Covers ban + limit + requirement? | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| ACTION ALLOWANCES | Low | Awkwardly | Reads as permissions |
| ACTION RULES | Medium | Yes | Confusable with RULES-as-mechanism |
| ACTION RESTRICTIONS | High | No (misses mandates) | Narrow |
| **ACTIONS** (the game property: *which actions are allowed, banned or required*) | **High** | **Yes** | Very short; needs the definition beside it |
| ALLOWED ACTIONS | High | Mostly | Mandates fit awkwardly |

**Recommendation (not frozen):**
- Internal name: **ACTIONS**. Definition: *which actions players are allowed, banned or required to use*. It parallels SPACE, NUMBERS, TIME, DIRECTION, SCORING as the **game property changed**.
- Keep "ACTION ALLOWANCES" only as a fallback.
- Coach-facing wording, if ever needed, can be plainer still: *"what they're allowed to do"*.

## 25. U12 volunteer translation

> **Why add a rule to a game?** To make something happen more often, like "no solo-ing this game" so they have to pass and move to help each other. Or to get loads of practice at something, like "left side only for ten minutes."
>
> **How do you know the rule is doing too much of the thinking?** Ask yourself: *is today about getting better at doing it, or getting better at choosing it?*
> - If it's about **doing** (a weaker side, a skill), a rule that makes them do it is fine.
> - If it's about **choosing** (when to pass, when to go), a rule that makes them do it has made the choice for them.
>
> **Watch for these signs:** passing just to reach your number, doing the "rule" thing when something better was on, or everyone arguing about what counts. That's the rule talking, not the game.
>
> **Then take it away and watch the normal game.** If they still do it at the right times, great. If they do it less, that might be fine too.

## 26. Simplicity test

**Internally** PáircVision distinguishes:
- RULES as a mechanism;
- the ACTIONS residue (which actions are allowed, banned or required);
- the DECISION ALLOCATION lens;
- the underlying lever effects (space, numbers, time, direction, scoring).

**A grassroots coach does not need any of those words.**

**Minimum distinction a coach genuinely needs (PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS, supported by the practitioner disagreement in §3 and §18):**
- **"Practise doing it" vs "practise choosing it."**
- It is what separates the two sides of the touch-limit debate, the weak-foot case, the FA No. 10 case and the mandate question.
- It sits **inside** the frozen question: "what am I leaving them to solve?" answered honestly tells the coach whether today is about doing or choosing.

**Flag:** "mechanism vs property", "action allowances", "legal-alternative test", "problem-amplifying vs solution-prescribing" are **research-level**. They should not reach coach-facing language.

## 27. Taxonomy recommendation (provisional; the final gate decides)

### **B — rename it and retain an equivalent concept**, alongside **D** for everything else a rule does.

1. **RULES as a mechanism (D).**
   - Practitioners use "conditions", "constraints" and "rules" for every kind of modification. Nothing in practitioner material argues for RULES as a peer lever.
   - Zone, participation, countdown, target and bonus rules stay with SPACE, NUMBERS, TIME, DIRECTION and SCORING.
2. **The residue survives, renamed (B).**
   - Practitioners clearly **do** change *which actions are available*: touch limits, no dribble, weak foot, no one-touch backwards, one-touch rondos, kick-vs-handball games.
   - Those changes are the subject of the liveliest practitioner disagreement (§3) and the clearest technique rationales (§13). No other lever describes them.
   - Recommended internal name: **ACTIONS** (*which actions players are allowed, banned or required to use*), under **CHANGE THE PROBLEM**.
3. **Not E (split further).** Splitting ACTIONS into "restrictions" vs "requirements" or "problem" vs "solution" is not supported: practitioners don't split them, and most rules sit on a continuum.
4. **Not A as named.** "ACTION ALLOWANCES" was not recognised by anyone and misdescribes requirements.
5. **Not F.** The practitioner picture is consistent with the scientific audit.

## 28. Synthesis questions

### When is removing a choice from players useful coaching?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Technique restriction produces technical-test learning (non-dominant foot). Tactical learning from removed choices: **UNKNOWN** |
| **Practitioner opinion** | When the choice is not today's objective: technique, repetitions, habit-breaking, restart or team coordination, safety, deliberately creating failure, exposing teammates' problems (RP-7, RP-5) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Useful when the coach **knows** which decision was removed and does not read behaviour under the rule as evidence of that decision (CF-1 v2) |

### When does a rule expose the problem, and when does it prescribe the solution?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | No study compares the two |
| **Practitioner opinion** | "Exaggerate the problem" vs "tell them what to do"; prefer rewards over requirements when the choice matters (P5) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Not two types: a **continuum**. Ask how much of the solution the rule supplies. Most rules both raise a problem's frequency and remove some of its answers |

### Can an artificial rule be excellent practice even though players should never behave that way in a match?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Yes for technique (non-dominant-foot tests). Unknown for tactics |
| **Practitioner opinion** | **Yes**: one-touch rondos, regain deadlines, weak-foot games, individual constraints and "judicious deviation" from realism are all used deliberately at elite level (RP-12) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Yes, **if its purpose is doing, not choosing**, or if it concentrates a problem whose answer is left open. The match behaviour to judge is the unconstrained one |

### What should a coach look for before deciding to keep, change or remove a rule?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Rules change acute behaviour, sometimes with more errors; individual responses vary widely (scientific audit) |
| **Practitioner opinion** | Watch the game first (7–10 minutes; P10); look for purposeless or forced actions (P24, P5); keep rules clear and refereeable (P22); change conditions when a few players dominate; use the game as a diagnostic (P7) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Three looks: **(1)** Is the rule producing the problem I wanted, or just the action I named? **(2)** Is anyone doing the rule-thing when something better was on? **(3)** When I take it away, do they still choose it at the right moments? And remember that doing it less can be right |
