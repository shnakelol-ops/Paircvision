# PáircVision Phase 1 — Lever 04: DIRECTION — Cross-Sport Practitioner & Coaching Intelligence Audit

**Type:** Practitioner and coaching-intelligence audit. Research only: no drills library, sessions, UI, product features or production code.
**Companion to:** [DIRECTION scientific audit](./phase1-lever-04-direction.md). This document does not repeat that review. Where it uses science, it points to it (candidates **D1–D12**).
**Frozen context:** [SPACE gate](./phase1-lever-01-space-final-gate.md) · [NUMBERS gate](./phase1-lever-02-numbers-final-gate.md) · [TIME gate](./phase1-lever-03-time-final-gate.md)
**Status:** v1 practitioner audit, September 2026. See the [DIRECTION Final Evidence & Falsification Gate](./phase1-lever-04-direction-final-gate.md), which governs PáircVision V1 where the two differ.

> **Question.** Why do coaches give practice a meaningful direction, and when do they deliberately take it away?
> More precisely: *what does changing the objective make players more likely to perceive and do, and what does that manipulation remove or distort?*
>
> **Standing rules for this audit**
> - Practice behaviour ≠ learning ≠ transfer.
> - Practitioner confidence is never upgraded to scientific evidence.
> - The default summary for anything in this document is: **"Common elite practice; transfer unestablished."**
> - The aim is a framework that is **more nuanced, not more certain**.

## 0. Method, access and evidence status

### 0.1 How this audit was done

- Targeted web searches (September 2026) covered:
  - Gaelic football and hurling: GAA Tobar / Games-Based Approach, Kinnerk, kickouts / puckouts, 2025 football rules;
  - soccer: rondos, positional play, third-man play, "sterile possession" critiques, England Football principles;
  - rugby union: gain line, go-forward, backward passing;
  - basketball: advantage, paint touches, drive-and-kick, ball reversal;
  - field hockey, ice hockey, AFL and American football;
  - transition and counter-attack evidence.
- **Access.** The network proxy blocked direct fetching of almost every page, including learning.gaa.ie, publisher sites, ResearchGate and PMC.
  - **No page in this audit was read in FULL TEXT.**
  - Every practitioner source is therefore **SEARCH-INDEX ONLY (SIO)**: we saw the search engine's summary of the page, not the page itself.
  - Research papers are **ABSTRACT ONLY (AO)** at best.
  - **UNR** means an author, date, figure or quote could not be confirmed.
- **Consequence.** Quotes below are reproduced as the search index gave them. Any quote marked UNR must be checked against the original before it is reused.

### 0.2 Evidence-status labels (per example)

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **RESEARCH-BACKED** | A peer-reviewed study supports the *behavioural* claim. It does **not** mean learning or transfer is shown. |
| **GOVERNING-BODY GUIDANCE** | GAA, FA, rugby, hockey or other federation coach-education material. |
| **ELITE PRACTITIONER METHOD** | Attributed to a named elite coach through a primary or credible secondary source. |
| **COACHING CONVENTION** | Widespread in coaching media and drill sites. No identifiable author and no evidence. |
| **PRACTITIONER OPINION** | One practitioner's argument or blog. |
| **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION** | Our reading. It is not a finding. |

### 0.3 Folklore labels (§22)

RESEARCH-SUPPORTED · PARTLY SUPPORTED · COACHING PRINCIPLE · SPORT-SPECIFIC CONVENTION · UNVERIFIED · CONTRADICTED.

### 0.4 Honest headline

- **There is a lot of practitioner reasoning** about why direction is used.
  - The most articulate reasoning comes from soccer positional play, basketball "advantage" thinking, rugby gain-line language, and AFL corridor and switch language.
- **Almost none of it is tested for learning or transfer.**
- **Gaelic sources on the *why* of directional design are thin.**
  - Tobar gives a generic Games-Based Approach rationale.
  - Kinnerk's co-authored research is about games-based coaching in general, not about direction.
  - We found **no primary source** in which Kinnerk explains a specific directional manipulation (§6).
- **The richest Gaelic material is match analysis of kickouts and puckouts**, not practice design.

## 1. Scope and what this document is not

- **It is not** a drill list. Practice structures are described only to the depth needed to explain *why* a coach uses them.
- **It is not** a restatement of the scientific audit. The scientific findings are cited by candidate number (D1–D12).
- **DIRECTION** keeps the narrow definition set in the scientific audit (§1 there):
  - DIRECTION = **objective structure**: what counts as success, and where.
  - Forward-only or pass-count restrictions are **RULES**.
  - Channels and zones are **SPACE**.
  - Cues and scanning are **INFORMATION**.
  - Where practitioners blur these, we say so.

---

## 2. Why this audit matters

The scientific audit found:
- targets change behaviour acutely (**D1**);
- possession games deliver more touches and passes (**D2**);
- **no study shows either format transfers better** (**D3**).

That leaves the *design rationale* almost entirely in practitioners' hands. This audit maps that rationale so the final gate can separate three things:

1. reasoning that matches the science;
2. reasoning that goes beyond it (untested but plausible);
3. reasoning that conflicts with it.

## 3. Example records (cross-sport)

Format per record: SPORT → GAME PROBLEM → PRACTICE STRUCTURE → DIRECTION / OBJECTIVE MANIPULATION → BEHAVIOUR SOUGHT → WHY COACH SAYS IT WORKS → WHAT PLAYERS MUST PERCEIVE → COACH OBSERVATIONS → COACH INTERVENTIONS → SIDE EFFECT / RISK → PROGRESSION / REMOVAL → SOURCE → EVIDENCE STATUS.

### E1 — Soccer: the rondo (non-directional by design)

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Sport | Soccer (Barcelona / Ajax lineage) |
| Game problem | Keep the ball under pressure; find the free player; react instantly to loss |
| Practice structure | Circle or grid; overload inside a boundary (4v1, 5v2, 6v2) |
| Direction manipulation | **None.** Direction is deliberately removed |
| Behaviour sought | First touch, pass speed, body shape open to the next pass, circulation speed, immediate counter-press on loss |
| Why coach says it works | Cruyff (reported): *"Everything that goes on in a match, except shooting, you can do in a rondo."* It gives high repetition of receiving and passing under pressure, with a built-in transition (whoever loses the ball defends) |
| What players must perceive | Where the pressure comes from; which passing lanes are open or closing; the defender's body |
| Coach observations | Touch quality; whether the player is "open" before receiving; ball speed; how fast the losing player reacts |
| Coach interventions | Touch limits (RULES), size of the circle (SPACE), numbers (NUMBERS) — **not** direction |
| Side effect / risk | No information about *where* the goal is. Defenders chase the ball rather than protect anything. "Everything except shooting" overstates it: the rondo also lacks **orientation to a target**, which is exactly what DIRECTION is about |
| Progression / removal | Practitioners describe rondo → positional rondo (players given positions) → positional game with direction, zones and neutrals → match |
| Source | Coaches' Voice (Cruyff quote, SIO); positional-play guides (SIO) |
| Evidence status | **ELITE PRACTITIONER METHOD.** Acute behaviour partly consistent with **D2** (more touches and passes in possession formats). Transfer UNKNOWN |

### E2 — Soccer: the positional game (direction added to the rondo)

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Sport | Soccer (*juego de posición*) |
| Game problem | Progress the ball through the opponent's lines in the right order |
| Practice structure | Rondo shapes placed inside a field with ends. Players occupy zones; neutrals create superiority |
| Direction manipulation | The rondo's circulation must now **end in** a line-break, a target player or an end zone |
| Behaviour sought | Circulate *in order to* find the free player further forward ("third man": A cannot reach C, so A → B → C) |
| Why coach says it works | The rondo teaches ball-keeping, but only with direction does keeping the ball gain a *reason*: to move the opposition until a forward lane opens |
| What players must perceive | Which line is broken; who the free player is; numerical, qualitative and positional superiority |
| Coach observations | Body orientation ("half-turn"); whether the ball goes forward when the lane opens; whether circulation is aimless |
| Coach interventions | Positions, zones and neutrals (SPACE / NUMBERS); target location (DIRECTION); questions about the free player |
| Side effect / risk | Position rules can make players wait in zones instead of reading the game. Superiority from neutrals is **given**, not created (NUMBERS gate: GIVEN ≠ CREATED) |
| Progression / removal | Remove neutrals, then remove zones, then play a match |
| Source | Coaches' Voice positional-play explainer; tactical guides (SIO) |
| Evidence status | **ELITE PRACTITIONER METHOD** / **COACHING CONVENTION.** Consistent with Coutinho et al. (2024): one target → better passing decisions (scientific audit §6) |

### E4 — Rugby union: gain line in a backward-passing game

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| Sport | Rugby union |
| Game problem | Every legal pass travels backwards (or flat), yet the team must gain ground |
| Practice structure | Small attack v. defence games (e.g., 4v2) with a gain line or a tackle line |
| Direction manipulation | Success = crossing the gain line or "getting over the tackle", with bonus points for clean breaks |
| Behaviour sought | Run onto the ball; take the ball at pace; keep feet moving forward "even if only diagonally" |
| Why coach says it works | Because passes cannot go forward, progress must come from the **carrier's running line** and **timing**. The gain line makes that progress visible |
| What players must perceive | Defender alignment and line speed; the space between defenders; support depth |
| Coach observations | Depth of the receiver; flatness at the moment of catch; whether contact happens beyond the gain line |
| Coach interventions | Score the gain line (DIRECTION); change defender numbers (NUMBERS); cues on depth and timing (INFORMATION) |
| Side effect / risk | A gain-line bonus can reward head-down carries into contact over passing into space |
| Progression / removal | Remove the bonus; judge by tries and phases retained |
| Source | Rugby Coach Weekly, "Go forward" principles and passing articles (SIO) |
| Evidence status | **COACHING CONVENTION** / **GOVERNING-BODY-ADJACENT** (RFU-qualified authors). No learning evidence |

## 4. Possession vs direction

### A. Non-directional possession: why coaches use it on purpose

Practitioners give these reasons (all **ELITE PRACTITIONER METHOD** or **COACHING CONVENTION** unless stated):

1. **Volume.** More touches, passes and receptions per minute.
   - Science agrees *acutely*: +38.7 % touches and +99 % passes in possession formats (Rumpf et al. 2026 review; **D2**).
2. **Isolating the "keep" problem.** Without a target, the only problem is retaining the ball. That lets coaches load first touch, body shape and pass speed.
3. **Spatial exploration.** With no target, players move around more of the space.
   - Science: no-direction formats gave the most exploration (Coutinho et al. 2024).
4. **Built-in transition.** In a rondo, whoever loses the ball defends immediately, so counter-press reactions are trained constantly.
5. **Intensity.** Heart rate, perceived exertion and mental challenge are higher (Rumpf et al. 2026), though enjoyment is slightly lower.
6. **Low organisation cost.** No goals or goalkeepers are needed, and it runs in a small area.
7. **Defensive pressing without a target.** Defenders practise pressing and cutting lanes.
   - Critics note that defenders chase the ball rather than protect anything (**D7**).

### B. Directional play: why coaches add a target

1. **The objective gives possession a reason.** Keep the ball *until* a better option opens (E2).
2. **Defence gets something to protect.** Defending becomes compactness and protection, not chasing (**D7**).
3. **Orientation information.** Players learn where danger and opportunity are relative to a target (**D8**, hypothesis).
4. **"Game-realism."** The most-cited reason (E3), and the least defined.
5. **Decision content.** Forward, sideways, back, shoot, kick or carry are now meaningfully different options.
   - Science: one target gave better passing decisions (Coutinho et al. 2024).

### C. Transitions between them

- The common practitioner progression is **possession → possession with direction (end zones or target players) → attack a goal → match** (E1–E3).
- A second, less-discussed pattern **alternates within one game**:
  - phases where the team keeps the ball, then must attack a target;
  - or bonus points for completing N passes *and then* scoring.
- **Evidence.** Neither progression order has been tested for learning. The ordering is **COACHING CONVENTION**.

### D. Is "possession without purpose becomes sterile" evidence or cliché?

**Verdict: CLICHÉ as a practice-design claim. It has a meaningful kernel as a match-tactics critique.**

1. The famous "sterile possession" criticisms (Mourinho, Trapattoni, Guardiola's reported dislike of "tiki-taka") are about **teams in matches** having the ball without threatening. They are **not** evidence about practice.
2. Applied to practice, "sterile" implies possession practice teaches nothing useful for scoring. **No study shows this.**
   - The only comparison evidence is acute.
   - Possession formats give *more* touches, passes, exploration and intensity (**D2**).
   - Directional formats give better passing decisions (Coutinho et al. 2024).
   - These are **different products**, not a good and a bad version of the same thing.
3. What is defensible: **a possession game contains no information about where to go**. Practising keep-ball alone cannot train orientation to a target. That is a statement about *what is absent*, not about sterility.
4. "Purpose" is also unfalsifiable as usually stated. Any possession can be called purposeful or sterile after the fact.

> **PáircVision position.** Possession practice has a purpose: retention, receiving, and pressing volume. Whether it transfers to attacking a target is **UNKNOWN**, as is whether directional practice transfers better (**D3**). We do not rescue the "sterile" belief.

### 5.2 Current rules change the directional problem (football)

From the 2025 Football Review Committee rules (sources: Leitrim GAA, Dublin GAA CCC2 summary, Irish Examiner; SIO). The rules' permanent 2026 form is **UNR** and must be checked against the current rulebook.

| Rule | How it changes the directional problem | PáircVision interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Kickouts must travel beyond the 40 m arc | The very short kickout into the small area is removed. The restart's first direction is now forced **outward**, into a larger contested area | Kickout-direction evidence from before 2025 may not transfer to current play |
| Minimum 3 outfield players in each half | Mass retreat is limited. There are always attackers ahead of the ball and defenders behind it | Forward options exist more often; "go forward" becomes more frequently *available* |
| Two-point scores from outside the arc (40 m) | The scoring objective splits into distance bands. "Forward" is **not always** better: moving inside the arc can *reduce* a score's value | A native **multiple-objective** structure (§14) |
| Solo / toe-tap quick restart after a foul | Faster restarts; less time for the defence to set | Rewards reading whether the defence is set (§16) |
| Advanced-mark advantage only if a score results | Changes the value of long forward kicks to marks | UNR detail |

**Key Gaelic difference.** The Gaelic objective is not one goal mouth. It is **a scoring range and angle around the posts, over and under the bar**, now with distance bands.
- Direction in Gaelic football is therefore toward **range + angle + time on the ball**, not simply "toward goal".
- In hurling, scoring range is very large. Much of the directional problem is **getting a free strike with time**, not getting closer.

### 5.3 What is missing

- **No Gaelic study manipulates direction in practice.** This is confirmed from the scientific audit.
- **No Gaelic coach-education resource we could access explains why or when to add or remove direction.**
- Kickout and puckout practice design is **not public**. Only match outcomes are.

## 7. Soccer: rondos, positional games and third-player play

### 7.1 "Why a rondo with no goal?"

The practitioner answer, reconstructed from E1 and E2:

1. **Density of the specific problem.** Receiving under pressure happens constantly in a rondo but only occasionally in a match.
2. **Separation of problems.** Direction adds decisions about *where*. Removing it lets the "*how* to keep the ball" problem be practised at high volume.
3. **Transition training.** The rondo's loss–regain rule creates constant reaction.
4. **Culture and identity.** In the Barcelona lineage the rondo is also ritual, a daily shared language (COACHING CONVENTION; not a learning claim).

### 7.2 What direction adds (without accepting "more representative" as sufficient)

"More representative" is **not** sufficient because it does not say *what information* is added. Direction adds:

1. **An orientation reference.** Positions, body shape and pass choices can be right or wrong *relative to a target*.
2. **Asymmetric value between passes.** A pass that breaks a line is worth more than a square pass. In a rondo all completions are equal.
3. **A defence that protects.** Defenders prioritise goal-side positioning, which changes which lanes open (**D7**).
4. **A reason to stop circulating.** Practitioners call this "the moment to go": circulation continues until a forward lane opens.
5. **Third-player play as a solution to a directional block.** A → B → C only matters when C is *further on* and direct access is blocked. Without direction, third-player play is just passing.

What direction may **remove or distort**:
- touch and pass volume (**D2**);
- spatial exploration (Coutinho et al. 2024);
- when targets are small or multiple, more individual play (four-baskets study, scientific audit §6).

## 8. Rugby: teaching progression when every pass goes backwards

- **The law separates ball direction from progress.** This makes rugby the clearest natural demonstration that **forward ≠ progress** (§11).
- Progress is taught through:
  1. **the gain line** as the visible marker (E4);
  2. **running lines and depth**: receive deep, arrive at pace, cross the line;
  3. **"go forward"** as a *team* principle: momentum and territory, not pass direction;
  4. **kicking**: the only way to send the ball forward, which is a deliberate trade of possession for territory.
- **Rugby's lesson for Gaelic games.**
  - A backward hand pass can be the action that lets the next carrier **arrive at pace** into space.
  - The value is in the *next* action, not the pass itself.
- **Status.** **COACHING CONVENTION.** Rugby-specific evidence on gain-line practice design was not found.

## 9. Basketball: moving the ball away from the basket to create a better attack

- The **kick-out** after a drive travels away from the basket, yet it is often the highest-value pass: it creates an open three-point shot (E7).
- **Ball reversal** to the weak side travels sideways or backwards. Practitioners claim it raises points per possession (**UNVERIFIED** practitioner analytics).
- The practitioner mental model is **advantage**: create → maintain → convert.
  - "Direction" here is **toward a better shot**, not toward the basket.
- **Lesson.** Distance to target is a poor proxy for progress when the defence is packed near the target. That condition applies to a Gaelic defence packed around the scoring zone.
- **Status.** **COACHING CONVENTION / PRACTITIONER OPINION.**

## 11. FORWARD vs PROGRESS

**Hypothesis tested:** *"Direction describes where an action travels. Progress describes what the action does to the game picture."*

### Evidence for the distinction

| Source | Supports the distinction? |
|---|---|
| Rugby: every pass goes backwards, yet teams progress (§8) | **Strongly.** Logically decisive: progress without forward passes exists |
| Basketball kick-out and reversal (§9) | Yes (practitioner) |
| Ice hockey regroup (E8) | Yes (practitioner) |
| AFL switch and lane change (E9) | Yes (practitioner) |
| Soccer analytics: D-Def disruption, space control (scientific audit §4) | Yes (research; **D4**) |
| Gaelic: longer possessions and more backward passing associated with scoring (scientific audit §14) | Consistent (association only) |

### Evidence complicating it

- **Counter-attacks against imbalanced defences are more effective** (Tenga et al. 2010).
  - When the defence is disorganised, *forward* and *progress* largely coincide.
  - The distinction matters most against **set** defences.
- **"Game picture" is undefined.**
  - Unless it is operationalised (for example defence displaced, free player created, scoring range or angle improved, time on the ball gained), "progress" can be applied **after the fact** to any action.

### Verdict

**USEFUL, with refinement.** A stronger wording:

> **Direction** describes where the ball or player travels.
> **Progress** describes whether the attack's scoring options improved or the defence's options worsened, judged against the objective *and* the defence's state.
> Against a disorganised defence, forward and progress usually coincide. Against a set defence, they often do not.

- **Status:** **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION**, grounded in practitioner consensus across four sports and partly supported by analytics (**D4**).
- **Not shown:** that coaching this distinction improves learning.

## 12. "Use the ball to improve the picture"

**Verdict: USEFUL SYNTHESIS. Parts of it are ALREADY ESTABLISHED ELSEWHERE, and it becomes TOO VAGUE without observable markers.**

- **Already established elsewhere:**
  - soccer positional play ("move the ball to move the opponent");
  - basketball "advantage" chains;
  - American football "stretch / conflict";
  - soccer disruption analytics (D-Def).
  - PáircVision would be re-labelling, not discovering.
- **Useful** because it:
  - unifies those ideas across sports;
  - explicitly legitimises backward and sideways actions;
  - suits Gaelic games, where hand-pass recycling is common.
- **Too vague** unless "picture" has markers a coach can see. Candidate markers (**PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION**, untested):
  - Did a defender have to move or turn?
  - Did a free player appear?
  - Did the ball carrier get time?
  - Did the scoring angle or range improve?
  - Did the defence have to reset?
- **Not misleading** in itself. It becomes misleading if used to justify **any** possession as "improving the picture". That is the "sterile possession" trap in new words.

## 13. Through / Around / Over

- **Where it lives.** England Football Learning names penetration as playing "through, around or over" the opposition (**GOVERNING-BODY GUIDANCE**). We could not trace its origin (**UNR**).
- **Practitioner usefulness:**
  - a simple **observation vocabulary** for a volunteer coach;
  - it prompts the question "which of the three was on, and which did we choose?";
  - it maps naturally to Gaelic games:
    - **through**: hand-pass or kick-pass lines through the defence; solo runs;
    - **around**: wide runs; switching; overlapping;
    - **over**: kick pass, long puck, aerial bypass of the press.
- **Limits:**
  1. It describes the **route**, not whether the route **improved the picture** (§11–12).
  2. It says nothing about **going back** (regroup, recycle), which rugby, ice hockey and basketball show is often the enabling move.
  3. "Over" in Gaelic games carries **contest risk** (marks and breaking ball) that the phrase hides.
- **Verdict.** **Observation language, not science** (**D9**). Suggested extension for PáircVision's vocabulary (**PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION**): *through / around / over / back-to-go*.

## 14. One objective vs multiple objectives

| Structure | Practitioner reason | Science | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| One target | Clear orientation; defence protects one place | One target → better passing decisions (Coutinho et al. 2024) | Predictable; the defence packs one area |
| Two or more targets (gates, multiple goals) | Teach switching and reading which target is open | Multiple targets → higher pace; small or multiple goals may make play more individual (scientific audit §6) | Players chase the easiest target |
| End zone / line | Encourages running beyond the line, not a single shot | Limited | Rewards any crossing |
| Intermediate objective (paint touch, gain line, corridor entry) | Makes a *sub-goal* visible | None direct | **Goodhart risk:** the sub-goal becomes the goal (E6) |
| Bonus scoring (switch = +1) | Rewards the desired behaviour | None direct | Players perform the bonus when it is not the right play |

- **Gaelic note.** Gaelic games already have **multiple native objectives**: goal (3 points), point, and two-point band (football, from 2025). The objective structure is multi-target by rule.
- Practice that collapses this to "score a goal in a small net" **removes** a real Gaelic decision: goal vs point vs 2-pointer vs keep the ball.

## 15. Direction and defence

- **The practitioner consensus is strong:** direction gives the defence something to protect.
  - soccer: compactness and protecting the central lane;
  - AFL: force the ball to the boundary and wall off the corridor;
  - hockey: protect the circle;
  - basketball: help defence protects the paint.
- In possession formats, practitioners say defenders "just chase the ball". This is plausible but **UNVERIFIED** as a learning claim (**D7**, partly supported).
- **Defensive direction is a design lever of its own.**
  - AFL "force to the boundary" and Gaelic press set-ups (kickout presses in E13) show the *defence* choosing where to send the attack.
  - This is under-represented in the scientific audit.
- **Kickouts and puckouts** are the clearest Gaelic case where **both teams hold a directional plan**:
  - the kicking team picks a zone;
  - the pressing team decides which zones to deny.
- **Status.** **COACHING CONVENTION** plus descriptive match analysis. No practice-design evidence.

## 16. Direction and transition (reorientation)

- When possession changes, the **target flips**. Practitioners emphasise:
  - **Immediate reaction.** Counter-press on loss (rondo culture, E1).
  - **Reading defensive balance.** Go direct against an imbalanced defence; keep the ball against a set one (Tenga et al. 2010; RESEARCH-BACKED, conditional).
  - **Restart speed.** The 2025 Gaelic quick-restart solo (§5.2) rewards the team that reorients first.
- **Practice implication (PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION).** Directional games with live transition train reorientation. Keep-ball trains loss reaction but **cannot train reorientation to a target**, because there is none.
- **Gaelic.** Turnovers and kickout wins are the main transition moments. Their outcomes are well described in match analysis, but **practice-design evidence is absent**.

## 17. Bad directional constraints

| Constraint | Practitioner criticism | Our classification |
|---|---|---|
| **Forward passes only / "no back passes"** | Bans the regroup, recycle and third-man move; in rugby it would ban every pass | Logically strong criticism (**D6**, PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS). Also a RULES manipulation |
| **Must switch before scoring** | Players switch when the near side is open | Same (**D6**) |
| **Minimum N passes before scoring** | Encourages killing time and exactly the "sterile" possession critics dislike; punishes the early forward ball against an imbalanced defence | PRACTITIONER OPINION; consistent with Tenga et al. (conditional value of speed) |
| **Must touch the paint** (basketball, E6) | Forces drives into a crowded paint | PRACTITIONER OPINION |
| **Gain-line bonus** (rugby, E4) | Rewards contact over passing into space | PRACTITIONER OPINION |
| **Offside-type rules in SSGs** | Rumpf et al. 2026 offside study: behaviour changes (scientific audit) | RESEARCH-BACKED (acute); not relevant to Gaelic (no offside) |

**Common thread.** A constraint that makes one solution **mandatory** teaches players to satisfy the rule, not to read the game. A constraint that makes a solution **more valuable** (bonus) or **more available** (space, numbers) keeps the choice with the player.
- **Status:** **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION.** It is consistent with constraints-led theory, but no comparative study exists.

## 18. What elite coaches observe

Collated from the practitioner sources. All are **ELITE PRACTITIONER METHOD** or **COACHING CONVENTION**. None is validated as a predictor of learning.

1. **Body orientation before receiving** (half-turn, "open"). Can the receiver play forward?
2. **Whether the defence moved.** Did a defender turn, step out, or shift across?
3. **The free player.** Was there one? Was it seen? Was it used?
4. **Ball speed and "stickiness."** Did the ball stop? (basketball: "indecision kills the advantage")
5. **Timing of the forward option.** Was the forward ball played when the lane was open, or too late?
6. **Depth and arrival speed of support** (rugby).
7. **Defensive balance at regain.** Set or unset? Did the choice (direct or keep) match it?
8. **Entry quality.** Controlled or speculative (ice hockey, hurling puckout retention)?
9. **Corridor vs boundary position** (AFL).
10. **Whether the rule is being "gamed"** — the bonus played when it isn't the best option.

## 19. Interventions: change what they SAY vs change the ENVIRONMENT

| Type | Examples (practitioner) | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **SAY**: questions and guided discovery | Tobar "Freeze and Fix"; "what did you see?"; "where was the free player?" | GOVERNING-BODY GUIDANCE. RQES 2025: players **disliked huddles** though they valued conditioned games |
| **SAY**: cues | "Open up", "arrive at pace", "play what you see", "switch it" | COACHING CONVENTION. Cue-based instruction can over-direct attention (TIME / INFORMATION gates) |
| **ENVIRONMENT**: change the objective | Add or remove targets; end zones; gates; scoring values | Acute behaviour changes (**D1**, **D5**) |
| **ENVIRONMENT**: bonuses | +1 for a switch, gain line, paint touch | No direct evidence; Goodhart risk (§14) |
| **ENVIRONMENT**: restrictions | Forward-only; pass counts | Risk of banning correct decisions (§17) |

- **Research on design capability.** Kinnerk et al. (2018) and Barrett, Kinnerk & Kearney (2025) report that coaches find game design difficult and want more sophisticated designs.
  - The bottleneck may be **environment design skill**, not knowledge of what to say.
  - This is a research observation about coaches, **not** a finding that environmental interventions outperform verbal ones for direction.
- **Comparative evidence (verbal vs environmental) for direction: UNKNOWN.**

## 20. Constraint removal and EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN

- **Practitioner progressions** (rondo → positional game → directional game → match; possession → directional in England Football guidance) are **progressions of *adding* direction**.
  - We found few explicit descriptions of **removing** a directional constraint and checking what remains.
- **Candidate mapping** (**PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION**, not validated):
  - **EXPOSE.** The objective is present but unrestricted, so the coach sees what players currently do.
  - **EXPLORE.** Bonuses, targets or spaces make an option more valuable or available (not mandatory).
  - **RELEASE.** Remove the bonus or intermediate target; keep the match objective.
  - **RETAIN.** Check later, in a reference game or match, whether the behaviour appears without the constraint.
- **What would count as evidence:** behaviour persisting **after** removal, on a **later** occasion, in a **reference game**.
  - The RQES 2025 GBA intervention used assessment games, which is the nearest Gaelic analogue.
  - It was not a direction-specific test.
- **We do not claim this sequence is validated.** No study tested it for DIRECTION.

## 24. Gaelic translation gate

Considers hand pass, kick pass, solo, long-range kicking, posts, goalkeeper, kickouts / puckouts, high press, aerial bypass, current rules and the large pitch.

| Concept | Verdict | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| AFL corridor / boundary / switch | **DIRECTLY TRANSFERABLE** | Large pitch, kicking, posts, marks and hand-ball equivalents. The scoring zone is angle-sensitive in both games |
| Counter-attack vs set defence (read defensive balance) | **DIRECTLY TRANSFERABLE** | A generic invasion-game logic. Gaelic turnover transitions are central |
| Kickout / puckout both-team directional planning | **DIRECTLY TRANSFERABLE** (native) | Already a Gaelic problem; rules changed it in 2025 |
| Forward ≠ progress (rugby lesson) | **TRANSFERABLE WITH MODIFICATION** | Gaelic allows forward passes. The lesson is that a backward hand pass *can* be the progress action, not that it always is |
| Basketball advantage chain / drive-and-kick | **TRANSFERABLE WITH MODIFICATION** | The solo and take-on collapse a defence, then a pass out to a shooter in range. The posts' range makes "kick-out to a shooter" real, especially with the 2-point arc |
| Ice hockey regroup | **TRANSFERABLE WITH MODIFICATION** | Recycling back (including to the goalkeeper) to re-enter with running support. Constrained by the 3-up/3-back rule and any current goalkeeper restrictions (UNR) |
| Controlled entry vs dump-in | **TRANSFERABLE WITH MODIFICATION** | Carried or passed entry vs a speculative high ball into the full-forward line; hurling breaking-ball dynamics differ |

| Field hockey elimination | **TRANSFERABLE WITH MODIFICATION** | Take-ons with solo or bounce; contact rules differ |
| American football stretch / conflict | **WEAK ANALOGY** | Set-play structure; only the language transfers |
| Rugby gain line as a scoring target | **WEAK ANALOGY** | Gaelic does not need to "cross a line" to progress: the ball can be kicked forward. It may be useful as a local tackle-line marker in contact phases |
| Offside-based SSG rules | **NOT TRANSFERABLE** | No offside in Gaelic games (the 3-up rule is a different, numbers-per-half constraint) |
| Single small goal as the only target | **NOT TRANSFERABLE** (as sole representation) | It removes the goal / point / 2-point decision and the over-the-bar scoring zone |
| Basketball "must touch the paint" | **WEAK ANALOGY** | A Gaelic "must enter the D / 21 m before scoring" rule would ban long-range scoring, which is central to the current game |

## 25. Final outputs

### A. Uses of DIRECTION (practitioner rationale)

1. Give possession a reason to end (the moment to go).
2. Give the defence something to protect.
3. Make pass value asymmetric (line-breaking ≠ square).
4. Create orientation information (where danger and opportunity are).
5. Train reorientation at turnovers.
6. Create intermediate objectives (gain line, paint touch, corridor).
7. Provide multiple-objective choices (goal / point / 2-pointer).
8. Rehearse restart plans (kickouts, puckouts) against a directional press.

### B. Why coaches use NON-DIRECTIONAL possession

1. Volume of receiving and passing.
2. Isolate ball retention from "where to".
3. Spatial exploration.
4. Constant loss–reaction transition.
5. High intensity in small spaces.
6. Pressing practice without a target.
7. Low organisation cost; daily team ritual.

### C. What adding an objective changes (acute, practitioner-observed; science where noted)

- **Fewer touches and passes; clearer decisions** (**D1**, **D2**; Coutinho et al.).
- **Defenders protect rather than chase** (**D7**).
- **Some passes become more valuable than others.**
- **Where players stand starts to matter** relative to the target.
- **Possible distortions:**
  - individualism with multiple or small targets;
  - gaming of intermediate objectives;
  - forward bias against set defences.

### D. Unsupported practitioner beliefs

1. Possession practice is "sterile".
2. Directional practice transfers better than possession.
3. "Don't go backwards" as a general rule.
4. Forward-only rules teach penetration.
5. Specific effect sizes for ball reversals or controlled entries from coaching sites.
6. "Game-realistic" as a sufficient justification.
7. "Everything but shooting" in a rondo.

### E. Useful cross-sport concepts for Gaelic games

1. Corridor vs boundary and switching (AFL).
2. Read defensive balance: direct vs keep (soccer, research-backed conditionally).
3. Forward ≠ progress (rugby).
4. Advantage: create → keep → convert (basketball).
5. Regroup / back-to-go (ice hockey).
6. Third-player play (soccer).
7. "Put a defender in conflict" (American football language).
8. Elimination as the purpose of the take-on (hockey).

### F. Concepts to reject (for Gaelic DIRECTION design)

1. Offside-derived rules.
2. The single small goal as the only target.
3. "Must enter the scoring zone before shooting" rules.
4. Mandatory forward-only or must-switch rules as default designs.
5. "Sterile possession" as a design argument.

### G. Through / Around / Over verdict

**Useful observation vocabulary; not science** (**D9**).
- It omits the **back-to-go** option and hides the contest risk of "over".
- Extend to **through / around / over / back-to-go** as PáircVision language (interpretation).

### H. "Improve the picture" verdict

**USEFUL SYNTHESIS**, mostly **already established elsewhere** (positional play, advantage, disruption analytics).
- It is **too vague** unless tied to observable markers:
  - defender moved;
  - free player created;
  - time gained;
  - range or angle improved;
  - defence forced to reset.
- It is misleading only if used to excuse any possession.

### I. Ten useful practitioner insights

1. The rondo trades orientation for volume, on purpose.
2. Direction gives possession a *stopping rule*.
3. In rugby, progress happens with zero forward passes.
4. The best pass in basketball often goes away from the basket.
5. Whether to go direct depends on whether the defence is set.
6. Defence gets something to protect only when there is a target.
7. Intermediate objectives make sub-goals visible, and can be gamed.
8. Switching is a response to a wall, not a ritual.
9. Short restarts only work with a credible long threat (hurling analysis).
10. Coaches find designing games harder than running them (Kinnerk et al.; Barrett et al.).

### J. Ten practitioner claims needing caution

1. "Directional games are more realistic."
2. "Possession without purpose is sterile."
3. "Play forward whenever possible."
4. "Don't go backwards."
5. "Everything but shooting in a rondo."
6. "More reversals = more points."
7. "Controlled entries = X % more shots."
8. "Short kickouts are best" (pre-2025 rules).
9. "Penetrate before you circulate."
10. "Guided questioning beats telling" — players in one Gaelic study disliked huddles.

### K. Questions the DIRECTION final gate must answer

1. Does any study show directional **or** possession practice improves **match** decision-making after the constraint is removed?
2. Can "progress" be operationalised in Gaelic games (markers in §12) reliably enough to observe?
3. Do Gaelic kickout and puckout findings survive the 2025 rules?
4. Does a mandatory directional rule reduce later decision quality compared with a bonus rule? (Untested; design question for PáircVision.)
5. Should defensive directional plans (force wide, deny zones) be treated as part of DIRECTION or a separate lever?
6. How should the Gaelic multi-objective structure (goal / point / 2-pointer) be represented in practice without distortion?
7. Where does "back-to-go" sit: DIRECTION, TIME, or both?
8. Can Kinnerk's actual design rationale be sourced from primary material (podcast transcript, GAA resource PDF)?
9. Is the rondo → positional → directional progression better than the reverse, or than mixing (never tested)?
10. Which parts of this practitioner audit should enter the frozen principles, and which remain vocabulary only?

## 26. Plain-language summary for a volunteer Gaelic coach

- **Keep-ball games are not "pointless".** They give lots of touches and passes and plenty of work. They just don't teach players *where* to go.
- **Adding a target changes the game.** Defenders start protecting something, and some passes become more valuable than others.
- **We don't know yet which kind of game makes players better in matches.** Nobody has tested that properly.
- **Going back isn't failure.** In rugby every pass goes back. In our game a hand pass back can be what lets the next runner hit the gap at speed. Ask "did that make things better for us?", not "did it go forward?".
- **Go direct when their defence isn't set.** When it is set, you may need to move the ball to move them.
- **Be careful with rules like "forward passes only" or "must switch before scoring".** They can make players do the wrong thing just to follow the rule. Rewarding the play you want usually works better than forcing it.
- **Our game has several targets:** goal, point, and now the 2-pointer. A game with one small net leaves out a real decision.
- **"Through, around, over"** is handy language for talking about attacks. Add "back to go" as well.
