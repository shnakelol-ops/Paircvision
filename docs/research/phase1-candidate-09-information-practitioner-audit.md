> ### ⚠️ ACCESS CAVEAT (read first)
>
> **Nothing in this audit was read in full, listened to or watched.** The proxy refused every practitioner source attempted in this and earlier audits (GAA Learning, Irish Examiner, The42, RTÉ, TGfU.info, FA, Medium, Substack, podcasts, YouTube).
>
> - All practitioner claims rest on **search-index summaries (SIO)** or research abstracts (**AO**).
> - Quotes are **as indexed**; search summaries may blend a source with the search engine's gloss. High-risk items are marked **UNR**.
> - Kinnerk's primary sources (2016 Munster Hurling Academy talk; TGfU.info interview; 2017 GAA conference slides) remain **not accessed**.
> - **Conclusions are provisional on access.**

> **Central question.** How do excellent coaches decide what information to give players, what to draw their attention toward, and what players need to learn to read from the game themselves?
>
> **Second question.** When does helping players understand the game begin to replace the very reading the coach wants them to develop?
>
> **Frozen:** *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"*
> **Under investigation only:** *"What in the game tells a player this is on, and is that still in my practice?"*
>
> **Standard:** MORE LOOKING ≠ MORE SEEING ≠ BETTER UNDERSTANDING ≠ BETTER DECISION · QUESTIONING ≠ DISCOVERY ≠ LEARNING · TELLING ≠ BAD COACHING.

## 3. "Triggers"

**What practitioners mean by a trigger is several different things:**

| Kind | Example | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|
| **A game situation** | Receiver takes the ball facing his own goal | **Game information** |
| **A team rule linking situation to action** | "When the ball goes back to their centre-back, we jump" | **Coach-supplied** (if–then rule, learned in advance) |
| **A moment** | "The second we lose it, we hunt" (Klopp) | **Game event** + team rule |
| **A clock** | Eight seconds to win it back (Rangnick) | **TIME** manipulation (in practice) |
| **A coach call** | "GO!" / whistle | **Coach-supplied**, live (substitution) |
| **A player call** | A teammate shouts "press" | **Player-generated** game information |

**Finding.**
- Practitioners use "trigger" for **all six**. The word **conflates** the game situation, the rule that says what to do about it, and the signal that starts it.
- **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION:** the useful separation is (a) **what in the game signals the opportunity**, (b) **what the team has agreed to do about it**, (c) **who or what says "now"**.
- Coach education teaches triggers mostly as **(a) + (b)**: a named situation plus a team response. The **recognition** of (a) is left to players; the **criterion** is supplied.

## 4. Pressing case study

| Approach | Who uses it (as found) | Supplied | Still to recognise | Purpose (as stated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Coach calls the press** ("Press!") | Common sideline behaviour; criticised as joysticking (Q11) | WHAT, WHEN (often WHO) | HOW | Immediate control; coordination |
| **Whistle / signal starts the press** in practice | Common practice structure (drill level) | WHEN | WHO, HOW | Repetition; clean starts (inferred) |
| **Predetermined triggers** taught as team rules | Coach education (Q6); elite teams (reported) | WHAT (press) + the **criterion** | **Detecting** the trigger live; WHO goes first; HOW | "At elite level, pressing is rarely random" (Q6) |
| **Coach tells players what to watch** ("look at his body shape") | Coach education (Q6) | Attention target | Interpretation, WHETHER / WHEN / HOW | Develop recognition |
| **Player calls the press** | Implied in communication material | (Supplied by a teammate, not the coach) | Everyone else: WHEN is supplied by the caller; the caller recognises | Coordination |
| **Players self-recognise** | Klopp's rationale (Q7) is a reason the moment is good; Q6 freeze-and-ask | Nothing (or only the principle) | Everything | Adaptability |
| **Countdown to regain** (Rangnick) | Q8 | A deadline (TIME) | WHO, HOW; WHETHER to press is fixed by the rule | Intensity, habit |

**Not ranked.** Each supplies different parts. Practitioners typically **combine** them: a team rule (criterion), players detecting, one player's call to coordinate, and freeze-and-ask to check recognition (Q6).

## 5. Coach call vs game cue

| Signal | Practitioner use found | Is the game trigger replaced? | Stated or inferable reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Whistle / "go" to start a phase | Common practice organisation (drill level; GAA Freeze-Fix structure) | Yes, for the **start** | Control, repetition, safety, clean restarts (inferred) |
| Verbal "press" / "drop" in games | Sideline behaviour (Q11) | Yes | Coordination / control; criticised when constant |
| Colour / number calls | Drill level | Yes | Reaction speed (weakly transferable: generic stimuli, scientific audit §6) |
| Coach feeds a ball to start | Common | Partly (the moment is chosen; the picture after is live) | Repetition of a situation (GAME MOMENT) |
| Freeze-and-restart | GAA GBA (Q15); trigger teaching (Q6) | No: it **stops** play **after** a game cue to ask what was seen | Checking recognition |
| Countdown clock | Rangnick (Q8) | No, adds a deadline | Intensity |

**Finding.** Practitioners **do** replace game triggers with calls in practice. **No accessible practitioner explains the choice** in terms of what it does to recognition. Reasons are **inferred** (control, repetition, coordination, early learning). **Not assumed poor coaching.**

## 6. Information substitution: practitioner usefulness

- **Direct discussion found?** **No** practitioner in accessible material names the concern that a whistle replaces the game's own signal.
- **Equivalent reasoning found?** **Partly**, in three places:
  - **Joysticking critiques** (Q11): players "turn to their coaches for what to do instead of their own brains or teammates". That is substitution **in matches**, stated as dependency.
  - **"Myths of scanning"** (Q2): practices without **visually rich** information limit what players learn to scan for. That is **missing** game information.
  - **Unopposed runs** (Q20): useful "without replacing the need for live decision-making".
- **Status:** **PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS**, consistent with practitioner concerns about joysticking and impoverished practice. **Not attributable** to any practitioner as a concept.

## 7. Highlighting vs answering

| Level (not a ladder) | Example | Practitioner support |
|---|---|---|
| Supplying the action | "Press now." | Common; criticised when constant (Q11) |
| Supplying the cue (criterion) | "Press when he receives backwards." | Trigger teaching (Q6); Read & React rules (Q18) |
| Directing attention | "Watch when he can't see forward." / "Look at the ball when he touches it." | Q1 (timing), Q6 |
| Asking players to interpret | "What did you see?" / "What tells you you can press?" | Q6, Q13, Q14, Q15 |
| Leaving recognition open | Play on; observe | GAA GBA (let the game run); "silent coaching" (Q12) |

**Finding.**
- Practitioner material **supports** these as distinct acts. Several sources use more than one in the same session (freeze → ask "what did you see?" → name the trigger).
- **Not a developmental ladder.** No source prescribes moving from "supply action" to "leave open" over time. Trigger teaching often **starts** by naming the criterion and **then** asks players to spot it.

## 8. Questions vs telling

| Position | Who | Reason given |
|---|---|---|
| **Heavy questioning** | Kinnerk (reported: "constantly asking questions"); GAA GBA; Morrison; Mulcahy ("two questions about everything", UNR) | Players become problem-solvers; ownership; understanding |
| **Questions made productive by shared principles** | Lemov (Q9) | Without principles, open questions make players **guess**, "which wastes time" |
| **Tell, then check** | Lemov (check for understanding); trigger teaching (name the trigger, then freeze and ask) | Efficiency; shared vocabulary |
| **Alternate by context** | Academy coaches (Q4): questions, phrases **and** video to build a common language | Shared understanding |
| **Players push back on questioning formats** | Gaelic U14 / U15 players **less receptive to huddles** (Q16) | Stoppages; format |
| **Less talk overall** | "Silence is Golden" (Q12); joysticking critiques (Q11) | Information overload under pressure; dependency |

**Why ask?** Understanding, ownership, checking what players saw, and building a shared language (as stated).
**Why tell?** Efficiency, shared principles, coordination, early learning, correcting misunderstanding (as stated or clearly implied).
**Disagreement is about *when*, not *whether*.** No credible practitioner found argues for questions only or telling only.

## 12. Scanning: practitioner folklore audit

| Coaching emphasis | Found? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequency** ("scan more", "check your shoulder") | **Common** | Drill and youth material |
| **Timing** (look away between touches; eyes on the ball when a teammate touches it) | **Yes, among the most sophisticated** | Q1 (Jordet): the high-frequency player who scanned **at the wrong time** |
| **What to look for** | Yes | Q1 (opponents, teammates, space); scanning "paired with a perceptual question" (as indexed) |
| **Practice must contain rich information** | Yes | Q2 (rondos insufficient); Q4 (opposed, chaotic practice best) |
| **Coaches unsure how to develop it** | Yes | Q3 |

**Finding.**
- Sophisticated practitioners **already distinguish** frequency from timing and content. Jordet's case is a direct practitioner-facing statement that **more scans ≠ better perception**.
- "Scan more" as a generic instruction is **folklore**; the scientific audit (IN-5) and Q1 agree.

## 13. "What are you looking for?"

- Practitioners who move beyond "scan more" name **when** (between touches), **what** (opponents' proximity, teammates, space) and **why** (to prepare the next action) (Q1, Q4).
- **Risk noted:** most of this is framed as **relations** (who is near, where the space is), not one cue. Pressing-trigger lists are **situations**, not body parts.
- **PÁIRCVISION INTERPRETATION:** practitioners support asking *what* players are looking for; they do **not** support a single correct cue.

## 15. Feedback: practitioner boundary

- Practitioners distinguish **before** (plans, principles, walk-throughs), **during** (calls, cues, sideline) and **after** (freeze, questions, video review), though not with academic terms.
- GAA GBA's **Freeze → Fix** is a response to what the coach **observed**, not a property of the practice.
- **Finding:** practitioners treat feedback as **what the coach does in response to what they see**. That supports moving feedback **out** of practice-design information into a later **coach-intervention** framework. **MULTIPLE-PRACTITIONER SUPPORT** (structure), consistent with the scientific audit.

## 17. Player communication

- Coaches **do** teach communication: calls, leadership, goalkeeper organisation (common coaching content; silent-game rationale that players "notice it's far easier and more effective with communication").
- Player calls are **player-generated game information** in competition (scientific audit §21).
- **Distinction supported:** player-generated (part of the game) vs coach-supplied (help).

## 20. Body orientation

- Practitioners use **"body shape"** for (a) a **player's own** receiving shape ("open up", "receive on the half-turn") and (b) an **opponent's** shape as a trigger ("receiving closed, facing the touchline"; Q6).
- Classification test:
  - Opponent's shape → **game information** ✓ (pressing triggers).
  - Own receiving shape → **skill / execution** ✓; requiring it → **ACTIONS** ✓.
  - Starting orientation → **GAME MOMENT** ✓.
- **Useful or artificially fragmented?** **Useful internally**: practitioners already use "body shape" in two senses (own vs opponent's), which the split mirrors. **Coach-facing:** "body shape" stays one everyday word.

## 28. Testing the candidate practice check

**"What in the game tells a player this is on, and is that still in my practice?"**

| Situation | Useful? | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Pressing | **Yes** | Exposes whistle / call substitution |
| Support play | **Yes** | Is there a real defender and ball-carrier whose movements signal where to support? |
| Shooting | **Yes** | Goalkeeper position, defender closing: absent in unopposed shooting |
| Transition | **Yes** | Real vs called turnovers (GAME MOMENT gate) |
| Defensive cover | **Yes** | Does the attacker's movement exist to read? |
| Kickouts | **Yes** | Opponent set-up, runner movement; a coach-nominated target removes it |
| Width | **Partly** | Useful if width should be **read**; irrelevant if width is a supplied shape |
| Overload | **Yes** | Is the overload visible and does it emerge, or is it announced? |
| Technical kicking practice | **Irrelevant** | Recognition isn't the aim |
| Set-piece rehearsal | **Misleading if read as "must preserve"** | The answer is deliberately "no, I'm supplying it" |

**Does it add something the frozen question doesn't?**
- **Yes, one thing:** the frozen question catches what the **coach tells**. It does **not** catch information that is simply **missing** from the practice with nobody supplying anything: an unopposed run, a rondo with little visual richness (Q2), a ball feed with no passer to read. Practitioners raise exactly this concern (Q2; Q4: scanning is best developed in opposed, chaotic practice; Q20: unopposed runs don't replace live decisions).
- **But** it is irrelevant or misleading for technique and set-piece rehearsal, and it risks being turned into a cue to **tell** players.
- **Recommendation:** **do not add** as a coach-facing question. Carry to the final gate as the strongest candidate for an **internal design check**, with a documented gap: *missing information that no one supplies*.

## 29. "Read the game"

How practitioners operationalise it:

| Operationalisation | Example |
|---|---|
| **Situations** (named) | Pressing triggers (receiver facing own goal) |
| **Relations** | Proximity of opponents, teammates' positions, open space |
| **Timing** | When to look (between touches) |
| **Rules linking reads to actions** | Read & React; team triggers |
| **Patterns / pictures** | "See and feel the pictures"; "pattern recognition" (Limerick, reported) |
| **Possibilities** | Where the space is before the move (Q19) |

**Finding.** "Read the game" is meaningful only when broken into **what situation, what relation, when to look and what it makes possible**. It should **not be frozen** as a phrase without those specifics. **No single correct cue.**

## 30. Information vs help

**Simpler architecture tested:** *the game contains information; the coach can provide help interpreting or acting on it.*

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Do practitioners separate what players read from what coaches say? | **Yes**: triggers vs calls; silent coaching vs joysticking; freeze-and-ask vs tell |
| Is coach help a property of the practice problem? | **No**: it is what the coach does (calls, questions, demos, feedback) |
| Can coach help change the problem? | **Yes**, when it **replaces** game information (calls, whistles) or when the practice **lacks** game information (unopposed) |
| Is game information already represented by existing levers? | **Mostly**: opponents (NUMBERS), space (SPACE), targets (DIRECTION), moments (GAME MOMENT) produce it |

**Verdict:** practitioners **support** the distinction **game shows / coach helps**. They do **not** treat "information" as a thing they manipulate on its own; they manipulate **opponents, space, moments and their own talk**.

---

## 31. Coach intervention

- Instruction, questioning, cueing, calls, demonstrations and feedback are **what the coach does**, before, during and after, in response to what they observe (Q4, Q6, Q9, Q11, Q12, Q15).
- They are **not** Phase 1 manipulations of the practice problem, **except** where a call **replaces** the game's signal (then the problem changes) or where the practice **omits** the information altogether.
- **Recommendation (explicit):** move coach-supplied information and feedback to a later **COACH INTERVENTION** framework. Phase 1 keeps only:
  1. the **substitution / missing-information** concern, as a design check; and
  2. **DECISION ALLOCATION + CF-1 v2**, which already read what any supplied information decides.
- **Not solved here.** Intervention (timing, question quality, amount of talk) is future work.

## 34. U12 volunteer translation

> **The game is always showing them things: who's near, where the space is, when the ball's coming.** Your job is to make sure your practice still has those things in it, and sometimes to help them notice.
>
> **Shouting "go now!" every time means they're watching you, not the game.** That's fine now and again, especially when you're setting something up. But if you always call it, you won't know if they can spot it themselves.
>
> **A good middle way: stop the game when the moment happens and ask "what did you see?"** Then tell them if they missed it. Asking and telling both have their place.
>
> **Keep it short.** Two or three things to look for, not twenty.

## 35. Simplicity gate

**Minimum useful distinction (tested):**
- **"What is the game showing them?"** vs **"What am I telling them?"**
- Practitioners already think this way (triggers vs calls; silent coaching vs joysticking).
- It fits **under** the frozen question: *"What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"* The first half is the coach's help; the second half includes what the game must show.

**One gap the frozen question does not reach:** information that is **missing** from the practice (nothing to read) when the coach hasn't said anything. That is the only reason to keep the candidate check alive (§28), and only as an **internal** design check.

**Terms that must not reach coaches:** specifying information, contextual priors, augmented cues, substitution, KR / KP, guidance, affordance.

## 36. Taxonomy recommendation (provisional; the final gate decides)

### **D — INFORMATION dissolves as a practice-design family.** Coach-supplied information (including feedback) moves to a later COACH INTERVENTION framework; game information is treated as a property produced by the existing levers.

**Why D rather than the scientific audit's C:**
- Practitioners do **not** manipulate "information" directly. They manipulate **opponents, space, moments, targets and their own talk**. Game information comes out of the existing levers.
- The problem-side residue the scientific audit proposed (deliberately changing what can be perceived) is **rare in practitioner practice**: occlusion is a lab / tool method; silent games are ACTIONS; called moments are GAME MOMENT plus a coach call.
- Coach-supplied information behaves like **intervention**: before / during / after, responsive to observation (IP-11).

**What survives in Phase 1 despite D:**
1. **The substitution / missing-information concern**, as an **internal design check** (candidate wording in §28), not a lever and not a coach-facing question.
2. **DECISION ALLOCATION + CF-1 v2**, unchanged, reading whatever the coach supplies.

**Why not B:** game information is not manipulated as its own property in practice.
**Why not C:** the residue is too thin to justify a named item, on practitioner evidence.
**Why not A:** "information" as one family mixes the problem and the help.
**E / F:** not needed; the split is clear.

**Disagreement with the scientific audit recorded:** the scientific audit leaned **C** (keep a small "information available" residue under CHANGE THE PROBLEM). The practitioner evidence leans **D**. The gate should decide whether the residue earns a name.

## 37. Direct answers

### What information should practice preserve if recognition is part of the learning goal?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Changing or removing the information source changes the task (scientific audit IN-1). Recognition-specific transfer: UNKNOWN |
| **Practitioner** | The situations and relations players must read: live opponents and their orientation, teammates' movement, the ball's path, space; opposed, chaotic practice for scanning (Q2, Q4) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Whatever in the game signals the opportunity (the situation), not a coach's stand-in for it |

### When is replacing a game cue with a coach cue legitimate?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | No study of its learning cost in invasion games |
| **Practitioner** | For coordination, clean repetitions, setting up a later decision, rehearsal, early learning (inferred from practice; not explained by coaches) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Whenever recognition of that moment is **not** today's objective. CF-1 v2 then limits what the practice shows |

### When should a coach tell rather than ask?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Explicit instruction speeds early learning in lab tasks; may be fragile under pressure (one study); questioning not isolated |
| **Practitioner** | Team structure, triggers as rules, restarts, opponent plans, shared vocabulary, limited time; when open questions would only cause guessing (Q9) |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | When the answer is not what players are meant to find today, or when finding it would waste the session |

### When should a coach point towards information rather than give the tactical answer?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Verbal cueing improved anticipation (handball, lab); highlighting ≠ supplying the decision (logic) |
| **Practitioner** | Trigger teaching: name the situation, then freeze and ask "what did you see?"; Jordet: tell players *when* to look |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | When recognising the moment is the objective but players don't yet know where to look |

### Can we meaningfully help coaches teach "reading the game" without pretending there is one correct cue?

| Layer | Answer |
|---|---|
| **Evidence** | Experts use global and relational information and context; one-cue instructions can mislead |
| **Practitioner** | Yes, when reading is broken into situations, relations, timing of looking and what it makes possible |
| **PáircVision synthesis** | Yes, by asking what in the game signals the moment and keeping it in the practice, rather than naming a single cue |
