# PáircVision Phase 1 — Lever 03 TIME: Final Evidence & Falsification Gate

**Parent document:** [`phase1-lever-03-time.md`](./phase1-lever-03-time.md) (v1 review, including the §16 CLT audit)
**Standard inherited from:** [SPACE final gate](./phase1-lever-01-space-final-gate.md) · [NUMBERS final gate](./phase1-lever-02-numbers-final-gate.md)
**Type:** Verification and falsification gate. Research only: no drills, sessions, UI or production code.
**Status:** Gate v1, September 2026. **Where this gate and the TIME review differ, this gate wins.**

> **Headline verdict.** TIME is **not one coherent lever**. It is a family of interacting manipulations. Most of them act on one emergent outcome, **usable time**, which is largely set by SPACE, NUMBERS and the opponent rather than by a clock.
>
> - **Fixed countdowns:** no transfer evidence.
> - **Touch limits:** no decision-learning evidence.
> - **"Creating time":** downgraded to coaching language, with a narrower defensible core.
> - **The right amount of temporal pressure for a player:** the evidence cannot prescribe it. **UNKNOWN.**

## 2. The twelve propositions: falsification verdicts

### T1. Clock time and usable time are different → **SUPPORTED** (mechanism); coaching application **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**

- **Directly supported (perception–action research):**
  - Affordances are perceived relative to the actor's own capabilities, and time-to-contact is action-relevant only when scaled to them (Fajen, 2005, 2007; lab / interception).
  - In invasion-game dyads, **interpersonal distance and relative velocity** govern whether the attacker gets past or the defender intervenes. Within about 4 m, relative velocity was the dominant control parameter in rugby dyads (Passos et al., 2008).
  - Expert–novice differences in cue pick-up speed mean the same clock time differs in usefulness (Mann et al., 2007).
- **PáircVision interpretation (untested):** a countdown sets only an *upper bound*, and usable time in games is mostly set by space, numbers, ball state, body orientation and support.
- **Strongest defensible wording:** *"The number of seconds on a clock does not describe how much time a player really has. That depends on how close and how fast opponents are, what the player can do, and where support is."*

### T2. TIME is not a single manipulation → **SUPPORTED**

**Where each variable belongs:**

| Variable | Home | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Usable time | **Emergent outcome** (of SPACE × NUMBERS × opponent) | Not directly manipulable; changed via other levers |
| Artificial clock / countdown / possession limit | **TIME (core)** | Directly manipulates clock time |
| Temporary opportunity windows | **TIME (core, as observation concept)** | Describes how opportunities change over time (§2 T7) |
| Player entry / recovery timing | **TIME ↔ NUMBERS (cross-link)** | Numerical relation changes through time |
| Restart timing | **TIME ↔ RULES (cross-link)** | Set by restart rules |
| Predictability of duration | **TIME** | Changes pacing (Ferraz et al., 2018) |
| Touch / action restrictions | **RULES (primary), cross-linked to TIME** | Mainly remove action options (§5) |
| Bout duration, work:rest | **Practice structure / load** (cross-link) | Evidence is mainly physiological; weak link to decision time |

**Test of "touch restrictions → primarily RULES, with a TIME interaction": SUPPORTED** (§5).

### T3. Less time does not necessarily produce better or faster perception

| Sub-claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Less time ≠ more scanning | **SUPPORTED** (match association) | Elite EPL players scanned **less** under tight opponent pressure (Jordet et al., 2020; 27 players, 9,574 possessions). *Association*: pressure and scanning both depend on the situation. No experiment shows added time pressure reduces scanning, and none shows it increases scanning. |
| Less time ≠ faster perception | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** | Time pressure speeds *responses* but lowers accuracy ("pressure-induced response acceleration"; basketball, *Front Psychol*, 2025). No study shows reduced-time practice trains faster perception. |
| Faster action ≠ better decision | **SUPPORTED** (acute) | Time pressure lowered decision / execution quality (Rochael & Praça, 2024; Camacho et al., 2021; basketball lab, 2025) |
| More urgent-looking play ≠ better speed of play | **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**, with indirect support | "Speed of play" is not operationalised in any study. NBA shot-timing mistakes lean towards impatience (Goldman & Rao, 2017). |

### T4. Artificial countdowns can worsen acute decisions and execution → **SUPPORTED (acute)**

| Study | Population | Design | Outcome measure | Finding | Limitations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rochael & Praça (2024), *IJSSC* | U17 soccer, n = 40 | GK+4v4+GK vs GK+5v4+GK, with or without a limit on offensive-action duration | Tactical decision-making and execution (coding instrument †) | Better decisions and execution **without** time pressure | Exact time limit UNRESOLVED; acute; one squad |
| Rochael & Praça (2025) | Same | Same | GPS load | No physical difference with or without time pressure | — |
| Camacho et al. (2021), *RQES* 92(3):443–452 | University students, n = 24 (not expert) | 3v3 basketball: free / 7-s possession / ≤3 passes / both | Mental load, affect, performance | All constraints ↑ mental load and **worsened** performance | Non-expert sample; acute |
| Basketball time pressure (*Front Psychol*, 2025) | Expert and novice college players | Lab / video decisions | Accuracy, RT, gaze | Accuracy ↓ under time pressure in both groups; novices worse | Lab task |
| Bredt et al. (2020), *Biol Sport* | U17 basketball, n = 12 | Reduced shot clock 3v3 | Physical | ↑ jumps; decisions not measured | Physical only |

**Contradictory search.** No study was found in which a countdown or possession-time limit **improved** decision quality. Birinci et al. (2026) found improved Stroop performance and passing with **2-touch** (not a countdown), and Stroop is not a game-decision measure.

### T5. Touch limits alter action possibilities, not merely time → **SUPPORTED** (with contradictions)

| Outcome | Consistent? | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| ↑ passing frequency | **Yes** | Systematic review (Rumpf et al., 2025); Rosten et al. (2025) |
| ↓ receptions / carries / dribbles | **Largely yes** | Free play ↑ receptions (Rosten et al., 2025). 1-touch excludes carrying by definition; 2-touch allows only receive-and-release |
| Technical success | **No: contradictory** | 1T lowest (index-level figures UNRESOLVED). 2T better than free play (Birinci et al., 2026) vs worse (JSMPF, 2025) |
| Tactical behaviour | **Little / scarce** | No effects on player distances across U9–U19 (Coutinho et al., 2022); the review calls tactical evidence "very scarce" |
| Physical intensity | **No: contradictory** | ↑ in adults (review); free play ↑ distance and accelerations in elite youth (JSMPF, 2025) |
| Decision quality | **Not measured** | — |
| Retained / transferred decision-making | **No evidence** | The only RCT (6 weeks, n = 47) measured **fitness** (limited touch ↑ anaerobic / sprint) |

**Reconciling the two-touch contradiction.** The studies differ in:
- population: elite youth (≈14.8 y) vs young males (age / level UNRESOLVED);
- format: 5v5 + 2 GK vs 4v4 (goal type UNRESOLVED);
- pitch size: UNRESOLVED in both;
- how "successful pass" was defined.

**The contradiction cannot be resolved at abstract level.** The defensible statement is: *the effect of two-touch on passing success depends on context and is currently inconsistent.*

### T6. Creating time and removing time are different coaching problems → **"Creating time" DOWNGRADED to PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC; narrower core PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**

| Behaviour | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Scanning / earlier information pickup** | Associated with more successful subsequent passing (Jordet et al., 2020; Hintermann et al., 2025: scanners ≈3× more likely to complete a pass). Trainable: scans ↑ and retained, elite only (Heads Up Girls!). **But training did not significantly improve the success of subsequent actions.** | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED:** *"Earlier information pickup is associated with better subsequent actions, and scanning frequency can be increased by practice in some players. It has not been shown that increasing scanning causes better play."* |
| Body orientation | Pass-feasibility models that include orientation (arXiv preprints) | **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** (modelling only; not peer-reviewed evidence of effect) |
| Positioning / separation / movement | Interpersonal distance and relative velocity govern dyadic outcomes (Passos et al., 2008; Vilar et al., futsal) | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED as mechanism**; coaching claim heuristic |
| First action / touch | Free play ↑ receptions (Rosten et al., 2025); no study of first touch "creating time" | **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** |
| Fixing / committing defenders | Decoy runners influence defenders (Sherwood et al., 2018); defender pressure can free passing lanes (basketball verbal reports, index) | **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** with plausible mechanism |
| Support, width / depth | Larger spaces ↑ inter-player distances and passing success (Lever 01) | **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** (space, not "player creates time") |
| Deception, deliberate delay | No direct study | **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** |

**Is "creating vs removing time" a real distinction?** As a **coaching framing, yes: PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS.** No study compares training to *create* usable time with training under *removed* time. **Required wording:** do not say "scanning creates time". Say *"looking earlier may let a player prepare an action before pressure closes the options."*

### T7. Temporary windows are meaningful game information → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** (mechanism); lifecycle model **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**

- **Support:**
  - Dyadic systems change state at critical values of distance and relative velocity (Passos et al., 2008).
  - Counter-attacks against unorganised defences are more effective (Tenga et al., 2010; Sarmento et al., 2018).
  - Counter-press success is predicted by regaining speed about 2 s after possession changes (Bauer & Anzer, 2021).
  - Top teams regain faster (Vogelbein et al., 2014).

  These show opportunities **exist, change and close**.
- **Not supported:** no study measures an "opening → available → closing → gone" lifecycle, or window durations, for passing lanes, support arrival, shots or short restarts.
- **No fixed number of seconds is implied.**

### T8. Fixed transition windows are not universally validated → **SUPPORTED**

| Claim | Source type | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Guardiola's Barcelona "6-second rule" (win the ball back within 6 s) | Media, coaching sites; no primary quote or document verified | **Coaching convention / anecdote** |
| Klopp "6-second" gegenpressing | Media often attribute Guardiola's rule to Klopp; attributions conflate the two | **Anecdote / internet folklore** (seconds figure); counter-pressing itself is a **tactical model** |
| Mourinho transition seconds | No source found | **UNRESOLVED; do not cite** |
| Rugby "quick ruck ball < 3 s" | Coaching benchmark. Media analyses (e.g., Six Nations) found faster rucks in try-scoring possessions (≈2.5 s) | **Coaching convention + descriptive match analysis** (not peer-reviewed; selection bias) |
| Basketball transition / "early offence ≈8 s" | Analytics sites use 8 s as a definitional window. Transition shots are more efficient (industry). NBA shot-timing mistakes lean towards impatience (Goldman & Rao, 2017, peer-reviewed) | **Analytics definition, not a rule of effectiveness** |
| Soccer "score within 6–10 s" | Analytics blogs | **Internet / analytics claim**; not peer-reviewed |
| Soccer match analysis | Counter-attacks more efficient (≈16% vs ≈10%, J Hum Kinet); speed at ≈2 s predicts counter-press success (a model feature, not a coaching rule); duration categories 0–10 / 11–20 s … (Sarmento et al., 2018) | **Match-analysis findings** (association) |

**Verdict.** **No universal transition threshold is scientifically defensible.** Match analyses support *fast exploitation of disorganisation*, not any particular number of seconds. Basketball's 5- and 8-second rules are **playing rules**, not effectiveness thresholds.

### T9. Faster is not inherently better → **SUPPORTED** (as a negative claim), with strong confounding caveats

- **Gaelic football:** longer possessions (31–90 s) were associated with higher scoring (sub-elite), and prolonged possessions with fewer attacking phases returned more scores (elite). See §4.
- **Soccer:** counter-attacks are more efficient on average, yet positional attacks remain the majority route and effectiveness depends on defensive organisation (Tenga et al., 2010).
- **NBA:** shot-timing errors lean towards impatience.
- **Confounders** (none resolved at abstract level):
  - team quality, opponent quality, scoreline and match period;
  - possession origin (turnover vs kickout);
  - start location;
  - defensive organisation (e.g., a deep blanket defence forces longer possessions);
  - tactical style;
  - **structural survivorship:** a possession that ends early often ends *because it failed*, so duration is partly an outcome of success.
- **Must not infer:** "long possessions cause scoring" **or** "fast attacks cause scoring".

### T10. Temporal pressure affects players differently by expertise / capability

| Part | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Expertise changes the response to temporal pressure | **SUPPORTED** | Novices decline more under time pressure (basketball lab); experts faster and more accurate (Mann et al., 2007); experts less affected by dual-task (Beilock et al., 2002); scanning training worked for elite, not grassroots; U9–U13 cope less with 1-touch (Coutinho et al., 2022, interpretation, UNRESOLVED) |
| We know how to prescribe the correct temporal pressure for each player | **UNKNOWN / not supported** | No study derives or validates individual prescriptions. The Challenge Point Framework is theoretical, with limited applied testing. |

### T11. Constraint stacking can create difficulty without useful learning → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED (difficulty, acute); learning UNKNOWN**

- **Direct sport evidence (acute):**
  - Camacho et al. (2021): a 7-s limit, a ≤3-pass limit, and both combined each raised mental load and worsened performance. **Whether the combination was worse than each alone is UNRESOLVED.**
  - More rule manipulations were associated with ↓ exploratory team behaviour (*Front Psychol*, 2019).
  - Pass restrictions raised mental fatigue (IJERPH, 2021).
  - Induced mental fatigue impairs decision-making (PeerJ review, 2021).
- **Not shown:** that stacking harms *learning*. No retention or transfer data.
- **CLT:** does not prove this. At most a THEORETICAL INTERPRETATION (§8).
- **Wording:** *"Adding several pressures at once makes practice harder and more mentally tiring in the moment, and has been linked to worse performance. Whether it helps or harms learning is unknown."*

### T12. Acute behaviour under temporal restriction is not evidence of learning → **SUPPORTED**

- Searched specifically for falsifying evidence: retention or transfer after countdown removal, touch-limit removal, or progressive temporal pressure. **None found.**
- The only longitudinal touch-limit study measured fitness.
- The scanning training showed retention **of scanning** in 4v4 tests, not of game performance, and not beyond 4v4.
- Registered touch-limit trial (NCT07395765): no results located.

## 5. Touch-limit final gate

| Condition | Temporal effect | Action-possibility effect | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unlimited | Player controls tempo | All actions available (carry, hold, fix, dribble) | Baseline |
| 3-touch | Mild bound on time on ball | Short carries possible; long dribbles removed | Rosten et al. (2025): restriction ↑ passes, ↓ receptions |
| 2-touch | Moderate bound | Receive + release only; carrying and fixing removed | Mixed technical success (Birinci et al., 2026 vs JSMPF, 2025) |
| 1-touch | Strong bound | First-time actions only | Lowest reported pass success (index-level, UNRESOLVED); interpreted as hard for U9–U13 |
| Conditional (e.g., limits in certain zones) | Varies | Varies | **No studies found** |

- **Temporal effect vs action removal.** Touch counts do not fix seconds: a player can take two slow touches. The consistent findings (more passes, fewer receptions) are **action-structure changes**.
- **Can the rule force rejection of the correct game solution?** **Logically yes.** If the best action is to carry, hold or fix a defender, the rule forbids it. **This has not been measured** in any study found. It is a **logical / practitioner concern**, consistent with the SPACE-gate principle about not systematically punishing good decisions.
- **Gaelic relevance (PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS):** solo, bounce, hand-pass sequences and carries are core. Soccer-style touch limits probably remove more of the real game in Gaelic codes.

**Recommendation: CROSS-LINK TIME ↔ RULES, with RULES as the primary home.**
- TIME retains only: *touch limits indirectly shorten time on the ball, and their effect on usable time depends on space and density* (Jäger et al., 2026: touch × player-number interaction).
- All other touch-limit content belongs to the future RULES lever.

## 6. TIME vs TIMING

- **What timing is.** The relation between a player's action and others' actions: a pass released as a defender commits, a support run arriving as the lane opens, a restart before the press forms.
- **Evidence.**
  - Coordination research shows timing *emerges* from coupled behaviour. Attackers try to destabilise, and defenders to preserve, in-phase relations. Critical distances and relative velocities trigger transitions (Vilar et al., 2014, futsal, *Eur J Sport Sci*; Correia et al., 2016, rugby; Passos et al., 2008).
  - Timing is therefore a **property of the player–environment relationship**, not a variable a coach sets.
- **What a coach can manipulate:** only the *conditions* that shape timing (defender start distance, entry delay, restart rules, space).
- **Recommendation:** treat TIMING as a **sub-concept of TIME**, specifically an **emergent relationship and observation target**. It is **not a separate lever.** Revisit only if later levers (e.g., INFORMATION, RULES) cannot house it. **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**, supported by coordination research.

## 8. Cognitive Load Theory: final disposition

| Boundary | What falls inside |
|---|---|
| **DIRECT SPORT EVIDENCE** | Expertise reversal in learning from **soccer tactical animations** (Khacharem et al.). Less time / pass limits / smaller pitch ↑ **perceived** mental effort, workload and fatigue (PSE 2022; IJERPH 2021; RQES 2021; *Sport Sci Health* 2026). Mental fatigue impairs decisions (review) |
| **EDUCATIONAL-PSYCHOLOGY TRANSFER** | Working-memory limits; element interactivity; "undesirable difficulty" under high interactivity (Chen et al., 2018); TBRS time–load link; motor-lab progressive difficulty |
| **THEORETICAL INTERPRETATION** | Artificial non-game constraints as "extraneous load"; Challenge Point nominal vs functional difficulty |
| **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** | Football "Cognitive Load Scale" (unvalidated framework); "touch limits / countdowns build decision-makers via CLT" |
| **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS** | Natural pressure more representative than clock pressure; speed of action ≠ speed of play |

**Verified must-NOT-claim list (all confirmed):**
- ❌ countdowns work because of CLT
- ❌ touch limits improve learning because of CLT
- ❌ arbitrary constraint stacking is "cognitive training"
- ❌ the football CLS is validated
- ❌ intrinsic / extraneous / germane is an uncontested current three-part model
- ❌ desirable difficulty is a CLT finding
- ❌ CLT proves speed of action vs speed of play

**What CLT legitimately contributes**
- **A. Designing the player's environment: very little.**
  - A reminder that the same demand is heavier for novices (consistent with T10).
  - Perceived mental load rises with temporal and spatial pressure (direct sport, acute).
  - No learning prescriptions.
- **B. How PáircVision teaches and explains material to coaches: legitimate and useful** (EDUCATIONAL-PSYCHOLOGY TRANSFER, plus direct soccer instructional-media evidence).
  - Segment and sequence explanations for novice coaches.
  - Avoid redundant or split information.
  - Adapt depth to coach expertise (expertise reversal).
  - This is CLT's home domain: instructed, biologically secondary knowledge.

- No study validates any method for setting individual constraint levels in invasion games, or tests coaches' accuracy in doing so.

- **9.1 does not support 9.2.** Knowing that difficulty varies by player does not tell anyone how to set it.

## A. What PáircVision can responsibly teach coaches today

1. **The clock is not the player's real time.** How much time a player has depends mostly on how close the opponents are, the space, the numbers and the player's own ability.
2. **"Time" in practice design is several different tools** (clocks, touch limits, late or recovering defenders, restarts, game length) that do different things.
3. **In the studies available, putting a clock on attacks made decisions and execution worse in the moment**, not better.
4. **Tight pressure tends to go with players looking around less, not more.**
5. **Touch limits mainly change what players are allowed to do.** They mean more passing and fewer carries. The effect on accuracy and fitness is inconsistent.
6. **Less experienced players are hurt more by time pressure.** The same limit is a different challenge for different players.
7. **Several pressures at once** (clock + pass limit, etc.) make practice more mentally tiring and performance worse in the moment.
8. **In Gaelic football match data, successful attacks are not mostly quick ones.** Winning the ball in contact and longer possessions have both been linked with scoring. This is a link, not a recipe.
9. **Seeing players succeed under a clock doesn't show they've learned to play faster.**

## B. Present only as a coaching option or heuristic

1. Encouraging players to **look around before they receive** (linked to better next actions; frequency trainable in elite youth; cause not shown).
2. Body orientation, first touch, fixing a defender and deliberate delay as ways a player may "buy time" (coaching language).
3. Counter-press and counter-attack **urgency after a turnover** (match-supported principle), **without** a fixed number of seconds.
4. Using late or recovering defenders so overloads close over time (untested).
5. Using touch limits knowingly, as a **rule** that removes options, not as "speed of thought" training.

## C. What remains a PáircVision hypothesis

- Creating time vs removing time as different coaching problems.
- The opportunity lifecycle (opening → available → closing → gone).
- Natural pressure being more representative than clock pressure.
- Speed of action ≠ speed of play.
- Timing as an emergent relationship rather than a lever.
- Touch limits removing more of the real game in Gaelic codes than in soccer.

## D. What PáircVision must NOT claim

1. ❌ Countdowns, possession clocks or touch limits **teach** better or faster decisions.
2. ❌ "Two-touch makes players think faster."
3. ❌ "Pressure makes players scan."
4. ❌ "Scanning creates time" (causal).
5. ❌ Scanning training improves passing. It didn't, in the one trial.
6. ❌ Any universal transition window (3 / 5 / 6 / 8 / 10 s), or attributing precise second-rules to Guardiola, Klopp or Mourinho as fact.
7. ❌ "Long possessions are better" **or** "fast attacks are better" (either direction, from association).
8. ❌ That PáircVision, or anyone, can prescribe the correct temporal pressure for an individual player.
9. ❌ Constraint stacking is "cognitive training", or proven to help or harm learning.
10. ❌ Every CLT claim listed in §8.
11. ❌ Any Gaelic or hurling evidence on time, touch or countdown **training** effects. None exists.

## E. Important contradictions

1. Two-touch passing success: better (Birinci et al., 2026) vs worse (JSMPF, 2025).
2. Touch-limit physical load: ↑ in adults vs ↓ in elite youth.
3. Coaching belief that pressure forces scanning vs match data showing less scanning under tight pressure.
4. Soccer "fast attacks more efficient" vs Gaelic "longer possessions linked with scoring" vs NBA "impatience errors". All are associations with different confounds.
5. The scanning intervention ↑ scanning but not success; scanning is linked to success in cross-sectional data.
6. 2-touch ↑ executive-function test performance acutely, yet time pressure ↓ game decision quality.

## F. Major unanswered research questions

1. Does practice with artificially reduced time improve decisions under natural match pressure after removal? **UNKNOWN.**
2. Natural (space / opponent) pressure vs clock pressure: which transfers better?
3. Do touch limits make players reject correct actions, and does this persist?
4. Does increasing scanning *cause* better play, and does it transfer to 11v11 / 15v15? Why do grassroots players not benefit?
5. How long do real opportunity windows last in Gaelic football and hurling (overloads, lanes, restarts)?
6. What explains the Gaelic possession-duration association once survivorship, territory and defensive set-up are controlled?
7. Does stacking constraints help or harm learning?
8. Can any method set individual temporal challenge reliably?
9. Do progressive temporal-pressure programmes improve retention or transfer in invasion games?
10. How can a volunteer coach tell **rushed** from **quick** play in real time?

## G. Plain-language TIME explanation for a volunteer Gaelic coach

> **How much time does a player really have?**
>
> Not what the clock says. It depends on how close the nearest opponent is, how much room there is, who's free, and how good the player is. Make the pitch smaller or add a defender and you've already taken time away, with no clock needed.
>
> **Clocks and countdowns.** In the research so far, putting a time limit on attacks made decisions *worse*, not better. Players rushed. We don't know whether practising against a clock helps in matches later. Nobody has tested it.
>
> **Touch limits.** "Two touches" mostly changes *what* players are allowed to do. There's more passing, and no soloing, carrying or holding the ball to draw a man. Those are often the right play in Gaelic football, so know what you're taking away.
>
> **Looking around.** Players who look before the ball arrives tend to do better with it. Stronger young players can be coached to look more, and it stuck, but it didn't make their passing better in that study, and it didn't work for weaker players.
>
> **Fast or patient?** After a turnover, getting at a disorganised defence quickly can pay off. But in Gaelic football match data, plenty of scores come from longer possessions too. There's no magic "score in six seconds". Look for *when* the chance is on.
>
> **Same drill, different player.** A time limit that suits your best players may swamp your weaker ones.
>
> **The honest test.** If they manage it under the clock, the drill is working. To see whether they've learned it, take the clock away, play a normal game next week, and watch: do they speed up when the chance is there and keep the ball when it isn't?

## H. Recommended frozen TIME v1 principles

1. **T1 (Usable time, not clock time).** The time a player really has depends on opponents, space, numbers, support and the player's capability. A clock sets only an upper limit. *SUPPORTED (mechanism).*
2. **T2 (TIME is a family).** Temporal practice design is several distinct manipulations: clocks, entry and recovery timing, restarts, predictability and opportunity windows. Touch limits live primarily under RULES, cross-linked to TIME. *SUPPORTED.*
3. **T3 (Less time ≠ better perception).** Removing time does not make players look more or decide better. Scanning tends to fall under tight pressure, and time limits worsened decisions acutely. *SUPPORTED / acute.*
4. **T4 (No countdown transfer evidence).** No evidence shows that clocks, countdowns or touch limits improve decisions under natural match pressure once removed. *UNKNOWN.*
5. **T5 (No magic windows).** Transition urgency is supported, but no universal threshold (3 / 5 / 6 / 8 / 10 s) is scientifically defensible. In Gaelic football, successful attacks are not universally rapid. *SUPPORTED (negative claim).*
6. **T6 (Earlier information, not "created time").** Earlier information pickup is linked to better subsequent actions and can be increased in some players. "Creating time" is coaching language, not established science. *PARTIALLY SUPPORTED / heuristic.*
7. **T7 (Pressure is player-relative).** The same temporal constraint affects players differently according to capability. We cannot yet prescribe the right amount for an individual. *SUPPORTED / prescription UNKNOWN.*
8. **T8 (Produced ≠ learned).** Behaviour produced because a clock or touch rule exists is not evidence it survives when the rule is removed. *SUPPORTED.*
9. **T9 (Timing is observed, not set).** Timing (when to pass, run or press) emerges from the interaction of players. Coaches shape it only indirectly through space, numbers, entry timing and rules. *PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS (coordination-research supported).*
