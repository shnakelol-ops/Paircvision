# PáircVision Phase 1 — Practice Design Lever #1: SPACE

**Type:** Evidence review (research only — no software, no drill library)
**Scope:** Gaelic football, hurling, soccer, rugby (union and league), basketball, field hockey, ice hockey, Australian football (AFL), futsal and related invasion games
**Status:** v1, September 2026. **Superseded in part by the [Final Evidence & Falsification Gate](./phase1-lever-01-space-final-gate.md)**, which verifies the † citations, records contradictions (notably between Gaelic football studies) and sets what PáircVision may and may not claim. Where the two documents differ, the final gate takes precedence.

> **North star:** PáircVision helps people coach the game. That means understanding what they are trying to teach and the game behaviours behind it, changing the practice environment on purpose, understanding why those changes work, and recognising whether players are actually learning.

## 0. How to read this document

### 0.1 Evidence labels

Every claim carries one of these labels. They are not interchangeable. PáircVision should never present a **[P]**, **[E]** or **[H]** claim to coaches in the language of an **[R]** claim.

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[R]** | **Research evidence.** Peer-reviewed empirical data. The strength qualifier (e.g. *"[R] – several studies, mostly soccer"*) matters as much as the label. |
| **[P]** | **Established coaching or governing-body practice.** Widely used, often endorsed by national federations, but not tested for the claimed learning effect. |
| **[E]** | **Expert opinion or theory-derived claim.** Follows logically from a theoretical framework (ecological dynamics, constraints-led approach, TGfU), but nobody has tested it directly. |
| **[H]** | **Hypothesis.** Our own inference for PáircVision. Plausible, untested, and must not be taught as fact. |

### 0.2 Confidence scale

| Rating | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **High** | Consistent across multiple studies and populations, including systematic review or meta-analysis. |
| **Moderate** | Consistent across several studies, but mostly one sport (usually soccer), one age band, or acute (single-session) effects only. |
| **Low** | One or two studies, inconsistent findings, or extrapolated across sports. |
| **Very low** | Theory or practice only, no direct test. |

### 0.3 The central caveat, stated once and applied everywhere

Almost all the research on space in invasion games is **acute and cross-sectional**. It measures what players do *inside* a game of a given size, usually in one session, usually with physical-load outcomes (distance, high-speed running, heart rate, RPE). Only a small minority of studies measure tactical behaviour. **Almost none measure learning**: retention after a delay, or transfer to a different format or to competition once the spatial constraint is removed.

So nearly every "effect" below is an effect on **performance during practice**, not on **learning**. Soderstrom & Bjork's (2015) review of the learning–performance distinction applies directly. Conditions that change or improve performance during acquisition can leave long-term learning unaffected, or even impair it, and the reverse can also happen. This single fact should shape how PáircVision talks about space.

### 0.4 Sport coverage (honest)

| Sport | Volume of spatial research | Nature |
|---|---|---|
| Soccer | Very large (hundreds of SSG studies; several systematic reviews and meta-analyses) | Physical load dominant; growing tactical (positional-data) work; very little learning work |
| Gaelic football | Small (a handful of SSG pitch-size studies) | Physical and technical counts; no tactical-positional or learning studies found |
| Hurling | Small (≈2 SSG studies) | Physical and physiological only |
| AFL | Moderate and growing | Physical and technical; some representative-learning-design (RLD) measurement work; one short intervention |
| Rugby league / union | Small–moderate | Physical load; some skill counts; strong dyadic "interpersonal distance" research |
| Basketball | Small–moderate | Court size vs player number; physical and technical counts |
| Field hockey | Small | Youth SSG task constraints |
| Ice hockey | Small peer-reviewed; large federation / analytics body | Youth cross-ice / half-ice formats |
| Futsal | Moderate | Strong ecological-dynamics dyad and decision-making work |

**Implication for PáircVision:** most statements about Gaelic football and hurling in this review are **extrapolated** from soccer and AFL. Wherever that happens, it is flagged.

## 1. Theoretical frame: what "space" actually is to a player

Before looking at manipulations, it matters what the lever acts on.

1. **Players do not perceive "pitch area". They perceive affordances**, meaning opportunities for action (Gibson, 1979). These are specified by *relative* information: distances and closing speeds between attacker, defender, ball and target, and the gaps between defenders ([E]; Araújo, Davids & Hristovski, 2006). Rugby union dyad studies show that attacker–defender *interpersonal distance* (and its rate of change) governs when a defender is beaten or a tackle is made (Passos et al., 2008) **[R]**. Rugby pass decisions are informed by how gaps between defenders open and close (Correia et al., 2011) **[R]**. Futsal shooting decisions depend on the goalkeeper and defender angles to goal (Vilar et al., 2013) **[R]**.
2. **Pitch dimensions therefore act indirectly.** Changing the lines changes the *distribution* of interpersonal distances, time-to-contact and local numerical relations. It does not directly set any of them. Two games with identical area can produce very different local spaces around the ball, depending on player numbers, rules, scoring targets and how teams choose to defend.
3. **Area per player (ApP)** (total area ÷ number of players) is the standard normalisation (Hill-Haas et al., 2011; Castellano et al., 2015). But ApP depends heavily on what you count:
   - whether goalkeepers are included;
   - whether the reference is the full pitch or the **effective playing space**, i.e. the area actually occupied by the players, which is far smaller than the pitch (Gréhaigne et al., 1997; Fradua et al., 2013).

   Different reference points produce very different "match-representative" SSG sizes. This is an **open methodological dispute**, not a settled number (see Q2 in §9).
4. **Space is a constraint in Newell's (1986) sense.** A task constraint interacts with individual constraints (speed, kicking range, perceptual skill, maturation) and environmental constraints (surface, weather, light). The same pitch is a different "space" for a U10 hurler and an adult inter-county hurler. **Action-scaled space** (e.g., relative to kick or strike range and running speed) is theoretically more meaningful than absolute metres ([E]; Buszard et al., 2016).
5. **Representative learning design (RLD).** Practice should keep the key information–movement couplings of the performance environment (Brunswik, 1956; Pinder et al., 2011). A spatial manipulation is useful for learning only if the behaviour it produces is driven by the same information the player will use in the real game. The TGfU literature makes the same point through the distinction between **modification-by-representation** and **modification-by-exaggeration** (Bunker & Thorpe, 1982; Thorpe, Bunker & Almond, 1986). Spatial manipulations usually *exaggerate* a problem. Exaggeration may speed up attunement to that information. It may also teach reliance on information the full game does not provide ([E]).

**Rough reference values (full-pitch ApP, all players on the field, approximate)** — for orientation only:

| Game | Approx. area | Players | Full-pitch ApP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gaelic football / hurling (≈137×82 m) | ≈11,200 m² | 30 | **≈370 m²** |
| AFL (oval, ≈160×130 m) | ≈16,000 m² | 36 | ≈450 m² |
| Soccer (105×68 m) | ≈7,140 m² | 22 | ≈325 m² |
| Rugby union (≈100×70 m, excluding in-goal) | ≈7,000 m² | 30 | ≈235 m² |
| Field hockey (91.4×55 m) | ≈5,030 m² | 22 | ≈230 m² |
| Ice hockey (≈60×26 m) | ≈1,500 m² | 12 | ≈125 m² |
| Basketball (28×15 m) | 420 m² | 10 | ≈42 m² |

Gaelic games give each player roughly as much or more space than soccer, and far more than most soccer SSGs (typical soccer SSGs are often <150 m²/player; Olthof et al., 2018). **[H]** Transplanting soccer SSG dimensions into Gaelic football or hurling probably compresses the game far more than coaches realise. This is especially likely to remove the long kick-pass, the long delivery into space and the kickout / puckout contest.

## 2. Manipulation-by-manipulation evidence

Format for each: **MANIPULATION → OBSERVED/EXPECTED EFFECT → MECHANISM → EVIDENCE → CONTEXT → POSSIBLE SIDE EFFECTS → CONFIDENCE**

### 2.1 Total area and area-per-player (ApP)

**Observed effects: increasing ApP (same numbers, bigger pitch)**
- ↑ total distance, high-speed running (HSR) and sprinting; ↑ heart rate (HR) and RPE, fairly consistently. **[R] – High.** The Praça et al. (2022) meta-analysis concluded that larger relative areas produce higher physical and physiological responses. Supporting studies include Rampinini et al. (2007), Casamichana & Castellano (2010), Castellano et al. (2015), Riboli et al. (2020) and Hill-Haas et al. (2011, review). Hurling 4v4: HSR distance rose from ≈198 m (small) to ≈254 m (medium) to ≈354 m (large pitch), with higher physiological and perceptual demands on the large pitch (Malone & Collins, 2017, *JSCR* 31(6):1518–1524; abstract-level). Rugby league: moving juniors from 400 m² to 2,800 m² increased distance covered by ≈15 m·min⁻¹ (Gabbett, Abernethy & Jenkins, 2012).
- ↑ passing success / ball retention. **[R] – Moderate.** Vilar et al. (2014) reported that pitch size changed the local opportunities to keep possession, pass and shoot. A 2025 study of 3v3 in youth soccer (J Sports Sci Med 24:522–531) found higher passing success on larger areas (75–125 m²/player).
- ↑ team dispersion (stretch, length, width) and inter-player distances. **[R] – Moderate** (soccer positional data: Frencken et al., 2013; Silva et al., 2014; Olthof et al., 2018).

**Observed effects: decreasing ApP**
- ↑ ball involvements per player, ↑ contested possessions and tackles, ↑ shots / scores per team. **[R] – Moderate.** Kelly & Drust (2009): more tackles and shots on smaller pitches. Adult Gaelic 4v4: the smallest pitch (40×20 m) produced more scores per team, more "no-score entries" and more possessions per team (Mangan, Collins, Burns & O'Neill, 2019, *IJPAS* 19(6):971–984). Elite adolescent Gaelic: the smallest pitch gave more possessions, hand passes, tackles and points scored (2019 **conference abstract** — not a verified full paper; see final gate). Fleay et al. (2018) found that AFL field size changed both technical and physical profiles (see full text for variable-level results).
- ↑ accelerations / decelerations (mechanical load), even though HSR falls. **[R] – Low–Moderate** (elite adolescent Gaelic SSG study; the soccer evidence is mixed — see Hodgson et al., 2014, where small pitches limited the chance to accelerate to high speed).
- ↓ **decision-making quality and shooting effectiveness** alongside ↑ dribbling success. **[R] – Low** (single study: J Sports Sci Med, 2025, 3v3 youth soccer. Its authors caution that the correlations with physiological strain are moderate and not generalisable).
- ↑ scanning frequency. **[R] – Low** (U12 English academy study comparing 7v7, 9v9 and 11v11: scanning rose as format size fell. The authors note that the *situations* scanned in small formats may not match the full game).

**Mechanism.** Smaller ApP shortens interpersonal distances and time-to-contact. The ball carrier gets less time, pressure arrives sooner, and more players are within the "zone of influence" of the ball. More involvements follow almost by arithmetic. Larger ApP lengthens run-ups and passing lanes. It affords HSR, long passes and switching, and gives defenders more space to cover.

**Counterintuitive and negative findings**
- In the sub-elite adult Gaelic 4v4 study (Mangan et al., 2019), **HSR and sprinting peaked on the *medium* pitch (60×20 m), not the largest (80×20 m)**, and **average and peak HR differed only trivially** across pitch sizes. This contradicts the simple "bigger = harder" rule. A plausible explanation (**[H]**) is that on very long, narrow pitches, play becomes stop–start or players stop committing to long recovery runs. **Contradiction (added at final gate):** on the *same* 40/60/80×20 m pitches, *elite* Gaelic players covered the most distance in all speed bands on the largest pitch (Malone, Solan & Collins, 2016). The non-linear result may be level-specific and is unresolved.
- More involvements do not mean better learning. Smaller spaces raise the *frequency* of actions but may lower their *quality* and the *representativeness* of the decision context ([R]-Low + [E]).
- Olthof et al. (2019) found that SSGs played at a match-derived ApP were **more tactically representative** of the 11v11 match than the conventional small SSGs most coaches use. A common default ("small is better for skill") may make practice *less* like the game ([R] – Low–Moderate).

**Context.** Mostly adult or late-adolescent male soccer. Gaelic and hurling studies are sub-elite or county-minor, 4v4 or small formats, and physical outcomes only.

**Side effects to watch.** Technical breakdown and frantic "hit-and-hope" play in small spaces with lower-skilled players. Accumulated accel/decel mechanical load. On large spaces: under-involvement of weaker players, turnover-free "walking possession", and high HSR load.

**Confidence.** Physical-load effects: **High**. Technical-count effects: **Moderate**. Tactical-representativeness effects: **Low–Moderate**. Learning effects: **Very low** (untested).

### 2.2 Pitch width

**Expected effect.** Wider → ↑ lateral team spread, ↑ use of wide channels, ↑ switching of play, ↓ defensive compactness (or ↑ defensive shuffling distances). Narrower → central congestion, ↑ direct / vertical play or ↑ contested play, ↓ switching.

**Mechanism.** Width sets the maximum lateral distance defenders must cover. The defending team's width limit determines whether a switch reaches an unmarked player before the defensive block shifts.

**Evidence.** Frencken et al. (2013) found a **"crossover" effect** in soccer SSGs: manipulating *length* produced changes in the lateral direction, and manipulating *width* produced changes in the longitudinal direction, with inter-team distances and surface-area differences varying across conditions. **[R] – Low (one study, small sample)**. This is important. It means width changes do not map one-to-one onto "the team plays wider". Teams reorganise their whole shape. Silva et al. (2014) found that team dispersion responded to field dimension and was moderated by skill level **[R] – Low–Moderate**.

**Counterintuitive / negative.** The user's concern that players may simply **"stand artificially wide"** is plausible, but we found **no study that tested it directly** **[H]**. The measurement tools exist to test it. Coupling analyses (e.g., relative phase between team centroids, lateral stretch relative to ball position; Frencken et al., 2011; Folgado et al., 2014) could show whether width is *coupled to the ball and the defensive shift* (functional width) or *static* (positional width).

**Context.** Soccer only. No Gaelic or hurling width-specific studies found. In Gaelic football, handpassing and the solo / four-step carry rule mean width may serve a different purpose. It is probably less about switching by kick and more about isolation and running lanes (**[H]**).

**Side effects.** Static wide players, fewer central combinations, defenders learning to "hold the middle" in ways that won't work against a real opposition shape.

**Confidence.** **Low.**

### 2.3 Pitch length

**Expected effect.** Longer → ↑ depth, ↑ running in behind, ↑ long passing / kicking, ↑ transition distances and HSR. Shorter → ↑ shots / scoring attempts from distance, compressed transition, ↑ pressure on restarts.

**Mechanism.** Length sets the distance between scoring targets. That shapes how long transitions last and whether defending teams can stay compact vertically.

**Evidence.** Frencken et al. (2013) (see crossover above). A study of pitch length in youth soccer (*Anales de Psicología*, 2017, 33(3), "Influence of pitch length on inter- and intra-team behaviors in youth soccer") (Castellano et al., 2017) found that lengthening the pitch (width fixed) increased team length, convex hull and stretch index, with little change in team width. That contrasts with Frencken's crossover effect (abstract-level). The adult Gaelic 4v4 study varied *only length* (40, 60, 80 m at 20 m width) and found the non-linear HSR peak described above. **[R] – Low.**

**Side effects.** Long, narrow pitches may turn practice into straight-line running races. Short pitches may reward shooting from anywhere, which does not represent shot selection in the full game.

**Confidence.** **Low.**

---

### 2.4 Long/narrow vs short/wide (aspect ratio)

**Expected effect.** Long/narrow → vertical, direct play, 1v1 duels in channels, depth over width. Short/wide → lateral circulation, switching, more width-based overloads, less penetration.

**Evidence.** Mostly inferred from the Frencken crossover work and the Gaelic length-only study. Many SSG studies change area without holding the ratio constant, so **ratio and area are usually confounded** (Clemente et al., 2021, umbrella review, notes heterogeneous reporting). **[R] – Low; mostly [E].**

**Crucial caveat.** Because of the crossover effect, coaches cannot assume "wide pitch = wide play" ([R]-Low).

**Confidence.** **Low–Very low.**

---

### 2.5 Congestion (density of players in a region)

**Expected effect.** High density → ↑ pressure, ↑ turnovers, ↑ close-control / hand-pass demands, ↑ scanning frequency, ↓ time on ball, ↑ risk of technical breakdown. Low density → ↑ time, ↑ passing range, ↓ pressure.

**Mechanism.** Density is the local version of ApP. What matters to the ball carrier is the density *near the ball*, not the average.

**Evidence.** Mostly indirect, through ApP studies (§2.1). Vilar, Araújo, Davids & Bar-Yam (2013) showed that **local numerical dominance** in sub-areas of the pitch was related to goal-scoring opportunities in professional soccer **[R] – Low–Moderate**. Congestion as an *explicit* manipulation (independent of total area) has barely been studied.

**Age note.** Very young players "swarm" the ball regardless of space. Enlarging the pitch does not by itself create spacing in U7–U9 **[P / E]**. Swarming reflects perceptual and attentional development and the attraction of the ball, not only the available area.

**Confidence.** **Low.**

---

### 2.6 Channels / corridors (lines marked on the pitch)

**Expected effect.** Visible lanes → players use them as positional reference points. Positioning becomes more ordered. Possibly ↑ width occupancy and ↓ adaptive movement.

**Evidence.** Coutinho et al. (2019) tested youth Gk+5v5+Gk SSGs with sectorial and corridor lines. Visual spatial references changed players' positioning and physical performance **[R] – Low (one study)**. The detail of the direction of effect needs full-text confirmation before PáircVision uses it.

**Mechanism.** Lines add *information*. They are not physical barriers. Players may position relative to lines rather than to opponents, teammates and the ball ([E]).

**Transfer concern.** In the full game there are no corridor lines. **[H]** If players organise around painted references, the behaviour may not survive removal because the information driving it disappears.

**Confidence.** **Low.**

---

### 2.7 Zones (restricted areas, positional zones)

**Expected effect.** Guaranteed width/depth structure, protected roles, fewer players converging on the ball.

**Evidence — negative finding.** Gonçalves et al. (2017) compared 10v9 large-sided games under three conditions: (a) players restricted to assigned zones; (b) players allowed into neighbouring zones only; (c) free movement. **Restricting spatial exploration "greatly impaired the co-adaptation between teammates' positioning" and reduced physical and physiological output** **[R] – Low–Moderate (one well-designed study; elite/sub-elite soccer)**.

**Mechanism.** Zone rules replace perception-driven positioning (responding to teammates, opponents, ball) with rule-driven positioning. Co-adaptation, which is arguably what coaches actually want, is suppressed.

**Context.** Soccer large-sided games. Relevant for Gaelic coaches who use "keep your zone / hold your line" instructions.

**Real-world natural experiment (Gaelic football).** From 2025, the GAA's Football Review Committee rules require a minimum number of outfield players to stay in each half, set a 40 m arc beyond which kickouts must travel, and award two points for scores from outside the 40 m arc. These are **zonal and scoring-area manipulations applied to the full competitive game** **[P – governing-body rule change; no peer-reviewed evaluation found yet]**. They offer a rare chance to observe how zone constraints reorganise play at scale. It also shows that zones can be *representative* when they are part of the actual game.

**Side effects.** Loss of co-adaptation, players waiting for the ball to arrive in "their" zone, reduced defensive recovery, lower work rate.

**Confidence.** **Low–Moderate** that rigid zones reduce co-adaptation. **Very low** on long-term learning effects.

### 2.8 Protected / free spaces (e.g., defender-free zones, build-out lines)

**Expected effect.** Guaranteed time on the ball in specific areas. Intended to encourage playing out from the back, receiving on the half-turn, or safe kickout / puckout receipt.

**Evidence.** Largely **[P]**. Examples include youth-soccer "build-out lines" introduced by national federations, and restart zones where opponents must retreat. **No peer-reviewed study was found that tests their learning effect.**

**Mechanism (theory).** Removing pressure reduces the perceptual–motor demand so a skill can stabilise (a *simplification*). Removing pressure also removes the information (defender approach) that the skill must be coupled to in the real game ([E]).

**Side effects (plausible).** Players learn to receive only when protected. Opponents learn to "wait at the line". Exploitation of the rule (e.g., deliberately slow build-up). **[H]**

**Confidence.** **Very low.**

---

### 2.9 Scoring-area location and size

**Expected effect.** Nearer or bigger scoring targets → ↑ shooting, ↓ build-up, ↑ direct play. Further or smaller targets → ↑ build-up, ↑ precision, ↓ shot volume.

**Evidence.**
- **Proximity-to-goal changes dyadic behaviour.** In attacker–defender dyads, behaviour changes with distance from the scoring target: defenders stay tighter nearer goal (Headrick et al., 2012) **[R] – Moderate (basketball / soccer-type dyads)**.
- Goal type changes load and play in hurling SSGs (Malone et al., *JSCR*, game design / goal type / player numbers study) **[R] – Low**.
- In U12 soccer competition, changing player numbers, goal size and field size together changed play actions (García-Angulo et al., 2019). **[R] – Low; manipulations bundled.**
- Goal-type changes (small goals vs goalkeepers vs floaters) altered team dispersion and shape (Castellano et al., 2016) **[R] – Low**.
- The GAA two-point arc is a scoring-area manipulation in the competitive game **[P]**.

**Mechanism.** The scoring target is the main attractor that organises team shape. Its location sets the "danger gradient" defenders respond to.

**Side effects.** Oversized targets reward low-quality shots. Undersized targets can produce sterile possession. Unrealistic shooting angles and distances can damage shot selection.

**Confidence.** **Low–Moderate.**

---

### 2.10 Multiple scoring targets

**Expected effect.** More targets → teams spread wider, ↑ switching, ↑ scanning for the "open" target, defending teams cannot compress around one target.

**Evidence.** Travassos, Gonçalves, Marcelino et al. (2014): moving from one to three scoring targets produced **greater distances between teams and more time spent in lateral corridors and defensive sectors** **[R] – Low–Moderate (soccer SSG)**. Timmerman et al. (2019), U14 field hockey: game type (normal, cage, possession, two-goals) and player numbers changed technical and decision demands **[R] – Low**. Travassos et al. (2012) showed that increasing opportunities for action (e.g., more passing targets) made futsal passing practice more representative of match passing **[R] – Low**.

**Mechanism.** Multiple targets multiply the affordances for scoring. Defenders must balance several threats, which creates space elsewhere.

**Side effects.** In the full game there is one target at each end. **[H]** Multiple targets may teach switching away from pressure without teaching penetration against a compact block. The skill of "unlocking" a crowded defence may be under-practised. Defenders may learn a spread shape that is wrong for one-goal games.

**Confidence.** **Low–Moderate** for acute behaviour. **Very low** for transfer.

---

### 2.11 Boundaries / touchlines

**Expected effect.** Hard boundaries (boards, walls, "cage") keep the ball in play and remove restarts. Soft or absent boundaries increase restarts or free-for-all use of space.

**Evidence.** Timmerman et al. (2019) included a "cage hockey" condition in U14 field hockey **[R] – Low**. Ice hockey is played with boards, so the boards are representative there. In other sports, rebounds off boards are a non-representative affordance ([E]). Touchline-as-defender behaviour (defenders using the line to trap) is widely coached **[P]**, but we found no study that manipulated it.

**Side effects.** Players use walls as "teammates". Loss of restart practice (sideline balls, 45s / 65s, marks). In Gaelic games and hurling, restarts (kickouts, puckouts, sideline cuts) matter a great deal, so removing boundaries may remove a whole contest **[H]**.

**Confidence.** **Very low.**

---

### 2.12 Manipulating space around the ball vs away from the ball

**Expected effect.** Around the ball: pressure and time-on-ball are directly shaped (e.g., a "no-tackle radius", a minimum defender distance, pressure-intensity rules). Away from the ball: support movement, off-ball runs and cover are shaped (e.g., zones only for non-ball players).

**Evidence.** The interpersonal-distance research (Passos et al., 2008; Correia et al., 2011; Vilar et al., 2013) shows that **local, ball-centred distances** carry the decision-relevant information **[R] – Moderate (dyads / sub-phases)**. Studies of pressure-on-the-ball rules in SSGs exist (e.g., Frontiers in Physiology, 2023, "effect of pressure on the ball") but are mostly physical-load outcomes. **No study directly compares on-ball vs off-ball spatial manipulations for learning.**

**Mechanism.** Most coaching aims (support, width, depth, third-man runs) are *off-ball*. Most spatial manipulations are *whole-pitch*. That gap is real.

**Confidence.** **Low** for the premise that ball-local distance is the key variable. **Very low** for any particular manipulation.

### 2.13 Spatial overloads / local numerical density

**Expected effect.** A local overload near the ball (e.g., 3v2 in a zone) → ↑ successful progression, ↑ pass options, ↑ practice of "exploiting the extra player". Defenders under-loaded → ↑ decisions about when to press or hold.

**Evidence.** Vilar et al. (2013): local numerical dominance was linked with scoring opportunities **[R] – Low–Moderate**. Numerical imbalance changes load (e.g., *Science & Medicine in Football* 2021, "influence of relative playing area and player numerical imbalance" †) **[R] – Low–Moderate for load**. Torrents et al. (2016) showed that changing the numbers of teammates and opponents changed exploratory behaviour **[R] – Low**.

**Counterintuitive.** Numerical superiority can **lower** the physical demand on the superior team, and it can reduce the need to create space because it is given. **[R] – Low + [E]**

**Side effects.** Players learn to exploit an overload they didn't create, rather than learning to create one.

**Confidence.** **Low–Moderate.**

---

### 2.14 Changing space dynamically during a game

Examples: shrinking or widening mid-game, opening a zone after an event, "earn more space" rules.

**Expected effect (theory).** Forces re-attunement to changing information. May promote adaptability rather than one fixed solution. This is consistent with variability-of-practice and nonlinear-pedagogy arguments (Ranganathan & Newell, 2013; Chow et al., 2016; Santos et al., 2018, differential learning in youth soccer) **[E]**.

**Evidence.** **No direct study of within-game dynamic spatial change was found** in any invasion sport. Hristovski et al. (2011) and Torrents et al. (2016) supply the theoretical and exploratory-behaviour basis, not a test of dynamic space **[E]**.

**Side effects.** Confusion, loss of tempo, players "gaming" the rule change.

**Confidence.** **Very low.**

## 3. Effects by coaching target

Summary of the direction of effect when **area / ApP increases** (reverse for decreases) unless another manipulation is named.

| Behaviour | Likely direction (↑ area) | Evidence | Confidence | Key caveat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Support** (distance and angle) | Support distances ↑; supporting angles may become less immediate | [R] soccer positional | Low–Mod | Better support is not guaranteed; it may just be *further* support |
| **Width** | ↑ lateral spread | [R] | Low | Crossover effect (Frencken 2013) — not 1:1 |
| **Depth** | ↑ with length | [R]/[E] | Low | Confounded with ratio |
| **Penetration** | ↑ opportunities to run in behind; fewer players in the way | [E] | Low | Penetration vs a compact block may be *less* practised |
| **Passing** | ↑ success, ↑ long passing | [R] | Moderate | Fewer passes under real pressure |
| **Carrying** | ↑ open-field carrying; ↓ carrying in traffic | [E]/[R] | Low | Gaelic solo and four-step rule interact with this |
| **Scanning** | Smaller formats ↑ scan frequency | [R] (1 study) | Low | Scanning situations may be unrepresentative |
| **Decision-making** | Larger may improve decision *quality* | [R] (1 study) | Low | Often confounded with physiological strain |
| **Defensive compactness** | Harder to stay compact | [R]/[E] | Low–Mod | Defenders may learn "space-filling" not compactness |
| **Pressing** | Harder, more running to press | [R] load | Moderate (load), Low (behaviour) | Pressing success ≠ pressing learned |
| **Switching play** | ↑ with width / multiple targets | [R] | Low–Mod | Switching that bypasses the "hard" problem |
| **Transition** | Longer transitions, more HSR | [R] | Moderate (load) | Short pitches exaggerate transition scoring |
| **Technical execution** | Fewer actions but less pressure on each (larger); more actions, more errors (smaller) | [R] | Moderate | Error ≠ bad if it is at the edge of ability |
| **Physical load** | ↑ distance, HSR, HR, RPE (larger); ↑ accel/decel (smaller) | [R] meta-analysis | **High** | Non-linear exceptions (Gaelic 4v4) |
| **Enjoyment / engagement** | Youth: scaled (smaller) formats ↑ involvement and self-efficacy | [R] review + [P] | Moderate (children) | Enjoyment evidence mostly from equipment scaling and federation data |

## 4. Age and skill differences

### 4.1 Children (≈U7–U12)

- **Scaling principle.** A systematic review of 25 studies (989 children) on scaling equipment and play area found that children **prefer scaled conditions and report greater self-efficacy**. Evidence that scaling improves long-term skill acquisition is thinner and heterogeneous (Buszard et al., 2016) **[R] – Moderate for preference / self-efficacy; Low for learning**.
- **Ice hockey.** In cross-ice / half-ice 8U–U9 games, NHL and USA Hockey analyses report roughly twice as many puck touches, five times as many passes received and six times as many shot attempts as full-ice games **[P – federation analytics, not peer-reviewed]**. Finnish and Swedish federation research on small-area games reports 5–8× more shots **[P – federation report]**. A Canadian national survey (n = 6,372) found parents' and coaches' **beliefs** were a key driver of whether they accepted half-ice formats (Lemoyne & Tardif, 2024) **[R]**. This matters for PáircVision: *adoption barriers are about beliefs, not evidence*.
- **Gaelic games.** The GAA's Go Games for children use reduced numbers and pitch sizes **[P]**. No peer-reviewed learning evaluation was found.
- **U12 soccer.** Modifying players, goal size and field size in competition changed play actions (García-Angulo et al., 2019) **[R] – Low; bundled manipulation**.
- **Developmental constraint.** Children's kicking / striking range, running speed and perceptual span are smaller. **[E]** Adult-proportioned ApP gives them "space" they cannot use. Very small ApP produces swarming and scrambles. The meaningful scale is *action-scaled* (relative to how far they can pass and how fast they move), not metres.

### 4.2 Adolescents (≈U13–U18)

- Olthof et al. (2018) studied U13–U19 elite soccer 4v4+GK on small (120 m²) vs match-derived large (320 m²) ApP. **Intra- and inter-team distances and tactical behaviour changed with age category**, as well as with pitch size **[R] – Low–Moderate**.
- Folgado et al. (2014) found age-related differences in team length, width and centroid distance in youth SSGs **[R] – Low**.
- **Maturation** (biological age) is a large confound. Early maturers dominate large spaces through speed. Small spaces may reduce the speed advantage and reward technical and perceptual skill instead ([H]; plausible, untested for space specifically).

### 4.3 Skill level

- Silva et al. (2014) found that field dimension effects on team dispersion depended on skill level **[R] – Low–Moderate**.
- Elite adults may keep technical execution intact in very small spaces where novices break down (**[H]**, supported indirectly by the 2025 J Sports Sci Med finding that decision quality fell on the smallest pitches with regional-level youth).
- **The "edge of ability" principle [E]:** a spatial constraint is useful when it places the task at a level where players can succeed some of the time. The *same* area can be "optimal challenge" for one group and "technical breakdown" for another. That is why fixed recommended dimensions are inherently weak advice.

## 5. Representative learning design and transfer

### 5.1 Why performance in the constrained game is not evidence of learning

1. **Learning ≠ performance** (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015) **[R] – High (general motor learning / cognitive psychology)**.
2. A spatial constraint may **produce** a behaviour through *rule-driven or geometry-driven* means rather than *perception-driven* means. Examples: width because the pitch is wide; scanning because opponents are always close. If the information that drives the behaviour in the constrained game is absent in the full game, the behaviour has nothing to couple to after the constraint is removed ([E]; Pinder et al., 2011).
3. **Exaggeration vs representation (TGfU).** Exaggerating a problem can speed attunement, but only if the *information* remains the same kind (e.g., a gap that opens and closes) and only its *magnitude* changes ([E]).

### 5.2 What the evidence on learning actually contains

| Study | Manipulation | Learning test | What it shows | Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonney, Larkin & Ball (2022), AFL amateurs, RCT n = 22 | 4-week SSG programme vs traditional drills | Pre/post kick proficiency and "skill adaptability" | SSG group improved kick proficiency and adaptability | SSG bundle (not space alone); assessment also SSG-based; short; no competition transfer |
| Olthof et al. (2019), elite youth soccer | Match-derived ApP vs conventional small SSG | Representativeness (SSG vs 11v11 tactical variables) | Larger, match-derived ApP made SSGs *more tactically representative* | Representativeness ≠ learning; cross-sectional |
| Práxedes et al. (2018), youth soccer | Nonlinear pedagogy programme (bundled constraints) | Decision-making and execution in games | Improvements, differing by game action | Space not isolated |
| Santos et al. (2018), youth soccer | Differential learning programme | Creative and tactical behaviour | Improvements | Space not isolated |
| Machado et al. (2019) | Street-football-inspired NLP | Tactical behaviour | Improvements | Space not isolated |

**Bottom line [R-based]:** **We found no study in any invasion sport that isolates a spatial manipulation, trains with it over weeks, removes it, and tests whether the target behaviour survives in the unconstrained game or in competition.** This is the single largest gap for PáircVision.

### 5.3 How researchers and coaches *can* determine whether behaviour survives

Methods established in adjacent literatures (motor learning, RLD, positional-data analysis) that could be applied:

1. **Retention test.** Re-measure the target behaviour after a delay (≥24 h, ideally ≥1 week) in the *same* constrained game. This separates temporary performance from relatively permanent change ([R] – motor learning convention).
2. **Transfer test to a standard reference game.** Measure the behaviour in an **unmanipulated reference format** before and after the block. Examples: a match-derived-ApP game, or a full-sided game. The reference must stay constant across time points.
3. **Competition transfer.** Track the same behaviour indicators in real matches, e.g., how scores are created, kickout / puckout zone outcomes, turnover location, support distances if tracked. PáircVision already collects match events that could serve as *coarse* transfer indicators (**[H]**; these indicators have not been validated as learning measures).
4. **Representativeness audit.** Compare collective variables in the practice game with the same variables in matches (Olthof et al., 2019). Use a structured RLD tool: Krause et al. (2018) for tennis; Browne et al. (2020) for an AFL framework measuring action fidelity and constraint interaction.
5. **Functional coupling, not frequency.** To tell "learned width" from "standing wide", test whether the behaviour is **conditional on the information that should trigger it**. For example, does the wide player's position change with ball side and defensive shift (e.g., relative phase between team centroids or lateral stretch; Frencken et al., 2011; Folgado et al., 2014)? Width that does not co-vary with the ball and the defence is positional compliance, not tactical learning ([E], methods [R]).
6. **Constraint fading.** Remove or relax the constraint progressively and observe *where* the behaviour degrades. The point of breakdown tells the coach which information the player was actually using ([E]).
7. **Control or comparison groups.** Without a comparison group, general improvement from match play, maturation or season effects cannot be separated from the spatial intervention ([R] – research design principle).

## 6. Cross-cutting counterintuitive and negative findings

1. **Bigger is not always harder.** Sub-elite adult Gaelic 4v4 HSR peaked at medium length (Mangan et al., 2019), although elite players on the same pitch sizes ran most on the largest (Malone et al., 2016). HR differences across pitch sizes were trivial ([R]).
2. **Smaller is not always "more skill".** More actions, but decision quality and shooting effectiveness can fall ([R]-Low).
3. **Conventional small SSGs may be *less* game-like tactically** than larger, match-derived ones (Olthof et al., 2019) ([R]).
4. **Zones can suppress the co-adaptation coaches want** (Gonçalves et al., 2017) ([R]).
5. **Width changes reorganise length and vice versa** (Frencken et al., 2013) ([R]-Low).
6. **More scanning in small games may be scanning for the wrong things** (U12 academy study) ([R]-Low).
7. **SSG demands are variable from session to session.** Repeating the "same" game does not guarantee the same stimulus (Dudley et al., 2024, rugby union SSG variability) ([R]-Low–Moderate). Any learning claim must allow for this noise.
8. **Adoption depends on beliefs, not data** (Lemoyne & Tardif, 2024) ([R]).

## 8. What this means for PáircVision (research implications, not features)

1. **Teach the mechanism, not a dimension.** The evidence supports "space changes interpersonal distances and time. That changes what players can perceive and do." It does **not** support prescribing fixed pitch sizes.
2. **Separate three questions for coaches.**
   - What did the space change *during* the game?
   - Did the behaviour survive when the space went back to normal?
   - Did it appear in matches?

   The research answers the first reasonably well, the second almost never, and the third never for space in isolation.
3. **Treat Gaelic football and hurling as under-researched.** Most transfers from soccer should be labelled as such.
4. **Physical load claims can be made with confidence. Learning claims cannot.**
5. **Use the natural experiment.** The 2025 GAA football rule changes (half-restriction, 40 m kickout arc, two-point arc) change space in the real game. That is a research opportunity for understanding spatial constraints in Gaelic football.

---

## 9. The 10 most important unanswered questions

These need resolving before PáircVision should *teach* coaches to use space as a lever.

1. **Does any spatial manipulation, isolated from other constraints, produce behaviour that survives in the unconstrained game and in competition?** No invasion-sport study has done this with retention and transfer tests.
2. **What is the "representative" area per player for Gaelic football and hurling?** Should it be normalised to the full pitch or to effective playing space? Does the answer differ by phase (kickout / puckout, set attack, transition)?
3. **How should space be scaled for children?** By metres, by action range (kick / strike distance, running speed) or by perceptual span? And what are the long-term learning outcomes, not just involvement counts?
4. **When a smaller space increases action frequency but reduces decision quality, which one drives learning?** Is there an optimal "challenge point" in space, and how is it detected for a specific group?
5. **Does width (or depth) produced by pitch shape become *functional* width?** In other words, is it coupled to ball position and defensive shift? How can a coach see the difference?
6. **Do visual spatial references (lines, zones, corridors) create dependence** that fails when removed? Is there a fading schedule that avoids this?
7. **Are ball-local manipulations more effective for learning than whole-pitch ones?** Examples: pressure radius, local overloads versus pitch size.
8. **Does dynamic, within-session variation of space improve adaptability** compared with a stable space, in team invasion games specifically?
9. **How do skill level and maturation moderate spatial effects?** Can a coach identify from simple match or practice observations whether a space is causing technical breakdown or productive challenge?
10. **What observable, low-cost indicators can a coach (or PáircVision) use to tell whether spatially induced behaviour has been learned?** Could PáircVision's existing match-event data (shot zones, kickout / puckout outcomes, turnover locations) act as valid transfer measures, or do they need positional data?
