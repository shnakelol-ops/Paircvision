## 0. The critical finding first

Phase 1 studied **how the practice problem is built** and **what can be inferred from what players do inside it**. It deliberately did **not** study **how the coach teaches inside that problem** (COACH INTERVENTION, deferred), **what is worth teaching** (objectives, game model, development aims) or **how learning is recognised over time** (no validated method exists in the evidence we reached).

A coaching philosophy needs answers to all three. So the honest expectation, confirmed below, is that Phase 1 has produced **a disciplined framework for designing practice and reading its results**, with a small number of stances that feel philosophical but are mostly **conditional** ("if the objective is X, then …") or **epistemic** ("you cannot infer Y from Z"). The verdict (§31) is **C**, with a clear route to B.

## 2. What is the coach's job?

| Candidate responsibility | Status | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Decide what part of the game matters | **Required but not established.** Phase 1 presupposes an objective everywhere and never researched how to choose one. GM-3: no evidence says how often any moment should be practised | GM-3 **[FROZEN]**; choice of objective **[UNKNOWN]** |
| Design a practice where that problem can occur | **Supported as a design act**: choosing the moment and building the problem | GM-2 **[FROZEN]**; SYN §3 |
| Manipulate the environment deliberately | **Supported** that each property changes the problem | N1, DIR-1, SC-1, AC-1, GI-1 **[FROZEN]** |
| Understand what each manipulation changes | **Supported for acute behaviour**; interactions and individual variation limit prediction | SYN §8–9 **[EVIDENCE]** |
| Decide what information / decisions to supply | **Supported as a way of reading practice**, not as a rule for what to supply | DA-1 (synthesis), CF-1 v2 **[FROZEN]** |
| Decide what players must perceive / solve | **Supported conditionally**: if recognition is the objective, keep what tells players it's on | GI-2 **[FROZEN]** |
| Observe behaviour | **Supported** that observation is needed; **what** to look for is partly heuristic | SPACE A4; C08 AC-4 **[FROZEN]**; observation markers mostly **[HYPOTHESIS]** |
| Intervene when appropriate | **Cannot be specified**: COACH INTERVENTION is deferred | SYN §13.1 |
| Remove support eventually | **Hypothesis**, and not universal: removal only matters where independence is the aim | EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN **[HYPOTHESIS]**; C08 §10–11 |
| Distinguish practice performance from learning | **The most strongly supported duty in Phase 1** | SPACE P9, N6, T8, DIR-4, SC-2, AC-2, CF-1 v2 **[FROZEN]** |

**What follows [INTERPRETATION]:** the coach's job, as far as Phase 1 can specify it, is to **choose a problem, build it deliberately, know what they have supplied, and not over-read what they see**. The two duties most people would call central to coaching, **choosing what to teach** and **teaching it**, sit outside Phase 1.

## 3. What is the player's job?

Phase 1 does **not** assign players a single job. It asks the coach to know **which** job a practice gives them.

| Player job | When Phase 1 treats it as the objective | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Execute | Technique, rehearsal, coordination, safety; legitimate on its own | AC-3, DA-3 **[FROZEN]** |
| Perceive / recognise | When recognising the moment is what the practice is for | GI-2 **[FROZEN]** |
| Choose | When the decision is left alive and is the target | DA-1; CF-1 v2 |
| Coordinate | When shared execution of a supplied plan is the aim | DA-3 **[FROZEN]** |
| Adapt / solve | Implied by "change the problem" levers; not measured as learning | **[UNKNOWN]** as learning |
| Explore | Never established as necessary | EXPLORE **[HYPOTHESIS]** |
| Remember / transfer | Desired; not demonstrated for any tactical manipulation | **[UNKNOWN]** |

**Players are not required to discover everything.** Phase 1 repeatedly rejects that ("players must discover every cue themselves" is on the C09 must-not-claim list; "discovery is better than instruction" likewise). **[FROZEN via must-not-claim]**

**Execution alone is a legitimate objective** whenever the supplied decision is not today's target. Recognition and choice matter **when they are the target**, and then the practice must keep them alive (CF-1 v2; GI-2).

## 4. What is the role of the coach's answer?

Phase 1's most repeated normative move is **refusing** "supplying an answer = bad coaching" (CF-1 v2 clause; DA-3; AC-3; GI-2; CO-2).

| Supplied element | Legitimate when… (from gates) | Conflicts when… |
|---|---|---|
| WHO (jobs, roles) | Coordinated execution is the aim (DA-3) | Self-allocation is what players should learn |
| WHAT (mandated action) | Technique reps, pattern rehearsal (AC-3) | Choosing that action is the objective (AC-2) |
| WHEN (calls, whistles) | Execution after the moment is the aim (C09 §11; C10 §9) | Recognising the moment is the aim (GI-2) |
| HOW (technique model) | Technique acquisition (AC-3) | Players are meant to find their own execution **[UNKNOWN whether this matters]** |
| Starting position | Selecting the problem (GM-2) | Creating that position is the objective (CF-1 v2) |
| Overload | Exploiting an advantage is the aim (N6) | Creating / recognising the overload is the aim |
| Tactical response / pattern / team structure | Coordination, opponent plans (DA-3) | Players are expected to generate responses **[UNKNOWN whether they can later]** |
| Transition moment | Transition execution (C10) | Transition recognition (GI-2) |

**How central is "What am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?"** **[INTERPRETATION]** It is the **hinge** of the whole framework: every lever, CF-1 v2 and GI-2 is a way of answering it more precisely. But note what it is: a **question that exposes a choice**, not a **rule that makes it**. It tells the coach to decide; it does not say what to decide. That is strong evidence for a framework rather than a philosophy (§21).

## 5. Does the framework favour games?

| Claim | Phase 1 position | Provenance |
|---|---|---|
| Games are generally superior | **Not established** | SYN §8: learning evidence absent for tactical manipulations |
| Practising through games is necessary | **Not established** | AC-3 legitimises isolated execution |
| Representative practice is preferable | **Only conditionally**: when recognition is the objective | GI-2; C09 must-not-claim "representative practice guarantees transfer" |
| Isolated technical practice is inferior | **Rejected** | AC-3; SYN §9.7 |
| Every decision should remain alive | **Rejected** | CF-1 v2 clause; C08 must-not-claim "preserving every decision is always desirable" |

**Precise position [INTERPRETATION built on FROZEN pieces]:** the framework does not favour games; it favours **matching the activity to what it is meant to develop**, and it requires that **if a decision or recognition is the target, the activity must actually contain it**. A game can fail that test (a coach calls every moment; an unopposed pattern looks match-like); a drill can pass it (execution is the target).

## 6. Technical practice

- **Coexists by design.** AC-3 **[FROZEN]**: restricting choice is legitimate when the removed choice isn't the objective; non-dominant-side restriction improves technical **test** performance **[EVIDENCE]**; game transfer **[UNKNOWN]**.
- **Phase 1 did not research technical skill acquisition.** It cannot say how kicking, hand-passing or shooting are best learned, how much repetition is needed, or whether variable or blocked practice is better. **[UNKNOWN]**
- **Does PáircVision care whether an activity is a "drill" or a "game"?** **[INTERPRETATION]** No. It cares about three things the label hides:
  1. what the activity is **meant** to develop;
  2. which **decisions** it leaves to players (DA-1);
  3. whether it **contains** the information those decisions depend on (GI-2).

  "Drill" and "game" are descriptions of format, and every format can be read through those three questions.

## 7. Constraints

**What Phase 1 concluded constraints are:**

| Property | What it changes | What it does not guarantee |
|---|---|---|
| SPACE | Involvement, contact, running, spread | Better decisions (SPACE C1) |
| NUMBERS | Involvement, shape, which solutions work | Recognition or creation of overloads (N6) |
| TIME | Pressure, urgency, load | Better perception or decisions (T3, T4) |
| DIRECTION | What is attacked and defended | That forward = progress (DIR-3); learning (DIR-4) |
| SCORING | What players attempt | Learning (SC-2); intended net effect (SC-4) |
| ACTIONS | Which actions occur | Choosing well without the rule (AC-2) |
| Event-contingent use | When a change applies | Different effect from continuous use **[UNKNOWN]** |

**Test statement:** *Constraints are tools for changing the problem players encounter, not teaching methods with guaranteed effects.*
- **Follows directly** from the frozen principles above. **[FROZEN in substance across gates; wording is INTERPRETATION]**

**Explicitly rejected:** *constraint applied → behaviour produced → learning achieved.* The first arrow is **[EVIDENCE]** (acute); the second arrow is **[UNKNOWN]** everywhere Phase 1 looked (SYN §8).

**A further observation [INTERPRETATION]:** Phase 1 began as a constraints investigation and ended by demoting constraints from "teaching levers" to "problem properties". The teaching question moved elsewhere (COACH INTERVENTION, objectives, learning assessment). That shift is the main intellectual result of Phase 1.

## 8. Representativeness (derived, not imported)

**From Phase 1 alone:**
- GI-1 **[FROZEN]**: changing, removing or replacing what players can perceive changes the task. A practice can look like a match and still lack the relationship a decision depends on.
- GI-2 **[FROZEN]**: if recognising a moment is the purpose, the practice must contain what tells players it's on.

**Philosophical implication [INTERPRETATION]:** the relevant question is never "does this look like the match?" but **"does this contain what the decision I'm targeting depends on?"** Resemblance is neither necessary nor sufficient.

**When it is not the objective** (from AC-3, DA-3, GI-2, CO-2): technique, tactical rehearsal, coordination, safety, deliberately isolated execution. In those, leaving the recognition problem out can be exactly right.

## 9. Player choice

**More choice is not always better.** Provenance: CF-1 v2 clause; DA-3; AC-3; C08 must-not-claim ("preserving every decision is always desirable"); C07 must-not-claim ("player freedom produces better decisions").

**Candidate position:** *Preserve the decisions you want players to learn to make; supplying other decisions may be legitimate.*

**Classification [careful]:**
- **Not a frozen principle** in these words.
- **Assembled from frozen parts:** CF-1 v2 (supplied decisions can't be inferred), GI-2 (preserve recognition when it's the aim), AC-3 and DA-3 (supplying is legitimate when not the aim).
- **Overall status: PÁIRCVISION SYNTHESIS / [INTERPRETATION].**
- **Hidden assumption not established by Phase 1:** that a decision practised is a decision **learned**. Preserving a decision makes learning it **possible within the practice**; Phase 1 has no evidence that doing so **produces** learning. **[UNKNOWN]**

## 10. Repetition

| Kind | Phase 1 position |
|---|---|
| Repetition of behaviour | Easy to produce (acute evidence everywhere) |
| Repetition of decision-making | Requires the decision to be left alive **and** its information present (DA-1, GI-2) |
| Learning | Not shown by either (SYN §8) |

**Example:** a coach-created turnover produces many repetitions of **transition execution** while removing **transition recognition** (C10 §9; GI-2). **Neither is wrong.** What follows **[INTERPRETATION]**: a coach should know **what** is being repeated, and should not count repetitions of one kind as practice of the other. "More repetitions mean more learning" is rejected (C10 must-not-claim).

---

## 11. Failure and error

**What Phase 1 can legitimately say:**
- Error-learning literatures (errorless, error amplification, workplace error management) **do not transfer** to tactical invasion-game learning on current evidence (C10 §20). **[EVIDENCE]**
- Letting play continue lets players **experience** what follows a mistake; that alone does not show they **learn** from it (CO-2). **[FROZEN]**
- Physical punishment for mistakes: no learning benefit found; qualitative youth harm evidence (C10 §17). **[EVIDENCE]**
- Gaming or unexpected behaviour tells the coach what the practice rewards, not that players failed (AC-4). **[FROZEN]**

**Not adopted:** errors are always good; failure drives learning; natural consequences teach; players must struggle.

**What remains [INTERPRETATION]:** error and failure are **information about the practice and the player**, not a mechanism Phase 1 can recommend.

## 12. Coach intervention (disciplined)

**Phase 1 cannot establish:** questions > telling; silence > instruction; guided discovery > explicit teaching; feedback frequency; freeze / replay superiority; demonstration strategy. All **deferred** (SYN §13.1).

**What Phase 1 can say about intervention without trespassing:**
1. Any intervention that **tells** players part of the answer is readable through DECISION ALLOCATION and limited by CF-1 v2. **[FROZEN]**
2. A coach's **call or signal** that replaces the game's own signal falls under GI-2 when recognition is the objective. **[FROZEN]**
3. "Set up the game and stand back" is not what credible practitioners describe (C08 §17; C09). **[EVIDENCE: practitioner, not learning evidence]**
4. Several popular intervention claims (external focus, self-control, reduced feedback frequency) have small or negligible bias-corrected effects. **[EVIDENCE]**

That is all. Anything about **how** to intervene is Phase 2.

## 13. What counts as success in practice?

**Attacked:** *"The behaviour appeared, therefore the practice worked."* Rejected by the strongest recurring Phase 1 finding (SYN §8–9).

| Observed | What a coach may reasonably infer | What they may not infer |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliance** (players did what the rule or call required) | The rule was followed; the practice produced the behaviour | That players understand when to do it (AC-2) |
| **Execution** (done well) | Execution under these conditions | That it holds under other conditions |
| **Successful behaviour** (it worked) | The practice allowed success | That success came from players' decisions if decisions were supplied (CF-1 v2) |
| **Appropriate choice** (chose it when it fit, among live options) | Players can make that choice **here, now, with this information** | That they'll make it later or in matches |
| **Learning** | Needs evidence beyond the session **[UNKNOWN how to obtain reliably]** | — |
| **Retention** | Needs a later look without the support | — |
| **Transfer** | Needs evidence in matches | — |

**Practical reading [INTERPRETATION]:** the most a single practice can show is **appropriate choice in that practice**, and only if the choice was live and its information present. That is a real and useful result; it is not learning.

## 14. What counts as learning?

**What PáircVision can safely say:**
- Learning is **not** shown by behaviour during practice. **[FROZEN]**
- Behaviour straight after removing a condition in the same session is a **useful observation**, not a retention test (C08 §11; SPACE B7). **[FROZEN / HYPOTHESIS as method]**
- A drop in behaviour after removal is not failure; appropriateness matters more than frequency (C08; SCORING). **[FROZEN]**
- Retention and transfer are **[UNKNOWN]** for every tactical manipulation audited.

**What it must not do:** adopt a definition of learning stronger than its evidence. **[INTERPRETATION]** A defensible working description is only: *learning is the part of what players do that persists and shows up appropriately when the practice's support is gone.* Phase 1 offers **no validated way** for a coach to measure that.

## 15. EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN (honest audit)

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Status | **[HYPOTHESIS]** throughout Phase 1; never upgraded |
| Does Phase 1 support keeping it? | Only as a way to **organise observation**; no learning evidence |
| Does anything contradict it? | **Partly.** (a) The one reference-game test found little average change after release. (b) AC-3 / DA-3 / CO-2 show many practices where release is **not** the aim (technique, rehearsal, coordination) |
| EXPOSE now means | Choose the moment and build the problem (GAME MOMENT + levers) |
| EXPLORE now means | Players work on the problem **with whatever support the coach chooses**. As named, it is **discovery-biased**: Phase 1 rejects "players must discover" and cannot yet say how coaches should teach within it |
| RELEASE now means | Look at the behaviour **without** a given support, **only** where independence is the objective. It does **not** imply constraints must always be removed (C08 §10: "rules should always be removed" rejected) |
| RETAIN now means | Look again later. It implies evidence a coach usually **cannot** collect rigorously; at best a structured second look |
| Recommendation | **MODIFY** (do not freeze): keep only as an **optional, conditional observation cycle** for practices where independence is the objective; replace the discovery connotation of EXPLORE; drop any implication of universal removal; describe RETAIN as "look again later", not as a test. Revisit after COACH INTERVENTION research, which may fill EXPLORE |

## 16. "Help Coach the Game": stress-testing the north star

> *PáircVision helps coaches understand what they are trying to teach, choose how to manipulate the practice environment to teach it, understand why that manipulation should work, and recognise whether players are actually learning it.*

| Clause | Phase 1 support | Issue |
|---|---|---|
| "understand what they are trying to teach" | **Not researched.** Phase 1 assumes an objective exists | Needs objectives / game-understanding work |
| "choose how to manipulate the practice environment to teach it" | **Partly.** Phase 1 explains what manipulations change; it does not show they teach | "to teach it" overstates; "to present the problem" is supported |
| "understand why that manipulation should work" | **Partly.** Phase 1 explains what a manipulation changes (acute) and what it cannot guarantee | "should work" implies learning mechanisms Phase 1 did not establish |
| "recognise whether players are actually learning it" | **Not supported at this strength** | Phase 1's strongest finding is that practice behaviour ≠ learning, and no validated coach-level learning indicator exists |

**More defensible wording (flag only; not a change to the north star):**
> *PáircVision helps coaches be clear about what they are trying to develop, build practices that actually contain that problem, understand what each change to the practice does and doesn't guarantee, and read what players do without mistaking it for learning.*

**Flag for the product owner:** the current last clause promises more than Phase 1 can deliver. It may become defensible only after learning-indicator research.

## 17. Does the framework teach a tactical model?

**No.** Nothing in Phase 1 requires possession or direct play, pressing or a low block, long or short kickouts, or any attacking shape.
- DIR-3 **[FROZEN]** explicitly refuses "forward = good".
- SC-3 **[FROZEN]** refuses "higher value = better option".
- T5 **[FROZEN]** refuses universal transition windows.
- The current Gaelic rules are a **reference**, not a model (SYN §3).

**The framework is tactically neutral [INTERPRETATION]:** it can serve any game model, because every principle is about **how the practice relates to the coach's objective**, not what the objective should be. The flip side: it **cannot tell a coach what to teach**.

## 21. Is this actually a coaching philosophy?

| Kind of thing | What it answers | Does Phase 1 answer it? |
|---|---|---|
| **Coaching philosophy** | What coaching is **for**, what players should **become**, how the coach should **relate to and teach** them, what counts as success | **Partly at most.** Success: yes (negatively). Purpose and teaching: no |
| **Practice-design framework** | How to build and vary the practice problem | **Yes, substantially** (GAME MOMENT + six levers + classification rules) |
| **Decision framework** | How to reason about choices and what can be inferred | **Yes** (DA-1, CF-1 v2, GI-2, kill tests, precedence rules) |
| **Learning theory** | How learning happens | **No.** Phase 1 refused to assume one and found learning under-tested |
| **Pedagogy** | How to teach (instruction, questioning, feedback, sequencing) | **No.** Deferred |

**Strongest case that it IS a philosophy:**
- It holds **values**, not just procedures: honesty about evidence, respect for decisions as something to be deliberately preserved when targeted, suspicion of dogma, and a refusal to confuse compliance with understanding.
- It has a **characteristic stance**: every method is conditional on purpose; the coach is accountable for knowing what they supplied.
- Many coaching philosophies are exactly this: a small set of values applied consistently.

**Strongest case that it is a practice-design / decision framework:**
- Every normative statement is **conditional** ("if recognition is the objective…") and never says **what the objective should be**.
- It is **tactically neutral** (§17) and **pedagogically silent** (§12). Coaches with sharply different beliefs about telling vs asking, or games vs drills, could all adopt it without changing those beliefs.
- Its "values" are mostly **epistemic** (what you can infer) rather than **educational** (what players should become).
- It **goes silent**, rather than breaks, on the core coaching acts (§22).

**Not decided here.** See §28 and the verdict (§31).

## 23. U12 volunteer test (five sentences)

> Before tonight, decide what you want them to get better at. Set the game up so that thing actually happens, and notice what you're doing for them (shouting when to go, telling them where to stand) versus what you're leaving them to figure out. If you want them to learn to spot something, don't spot it for them; if you just want them to practise doing it, it's fine to set it up for them. When they do it in training, that's a good sign, but it doesn't mean they've got it yet. Watch next week and in matches.

**Test:** a parent could act on this tonight. It changes **one** habit: noticing what they supply. Everything else stays theirs to decide, which is both its strength (usable) and its limit (it doesn't tell them how to teach).

## 24. Experienced senior coach test (≤ 250 words)

> Every change you make to a practice (pitch size, numbers, clocks, targets, scoring, what players are allowed to do, and which moment you start from) changes the problem your players face. None of those changes teaches on its own, and none has a proven carry-over into matches. The evidence is rich on what they do to behaviour in the session and nearly empty on what sticks.
>
> So the discipline is this. Be explicit about what you're developing. Then ask of every design and every intervention: what am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve? Supplying the answer is often right: set plays, pressing structures, technique reps, opponent plans. But whatever you supplied, you can't read back as their decision. If recognition is what you want (seeing the trigger, spotting the overload), the practice has to contain what shows it. A whistle, a called turnover or an unopposed run removes it, even when nobody says a word.
>
> Read results with the same discipline. Compliance isn't understanding; success under a supplied condition isn't choice; a good session isn't learning. A drop in the behaviour when you take the condition away isn't failure either; judge appropriateness, not frequency. The most a session can show is players choosing well, here, with that information.
>
> What this doesn't give you yet: how to teach inside the practice (tell, ask, show, freeze), how to judge learning over a season, or what's worth teaching. Those are the next questions, not settled ones.

**Test:** depth survives; the admission of what's missing is part of its credibility.

## 25. Observable changes in a coach's own behaviour (proposed, not validated)

If a coach adopted the framework for a season, these would be **plausible signs** it was influencing them **[INTERPRETATION; unvalidated]**:
1. Each practice is introduced with a stated **objective**, and the design choices can be traced to it.
2. **Fewer arbitrary constraints**: conditions have a reason the coach can say.
3. The coach can say, for any activity, **what they are supplying** and what players decide.
4. **Fewer claims that a session "worked"** because the behaviour appeared; more "they did it under these conditions".
5. **Deliberate choices about calls and whistles**: using them for execution practices, withholding them when recognition is the aim.
6. Some behaviours are **checked again without the support**, and again **later**, where independence matters.
7. Less reliance on **folklore** ("two-touch makes them think faster", "more scanning").
8. **No automatic preference** for games or drills; the format is justified by the objective.
9. **No physical punishment** for mistakes (outside the framework, but its evidence notes point that way).

## 27. Possible philosophy: blind derivation (strongest account, ~450 words)

> **Coaching, on this view, begins with a decision the coach must own: what am I trying to develop?** Nothing in the evidence makes that choice for them, and the view is honest that it can't.
>
> **The coach's role** is to build a practice in which that thing can happen. They choose which moment of the game players start from and meet, and they shape the problem by changing space, numbers, time, targets, what outcomes are worth and what players are allowed to do. Each change reliably alters what players do in the session. None of them teaches on its own, and none has shown that what it produces carries into matches.
>
> **Instruction and supplied answers are not the enemy.** Placing players, calling moments, assigning jobs, restricting actions and rehearsing patterns are legitimate whenever the supplied decision is not what the practice is for: technique, coordination, set structures, safety. **Technical work** belongs here without apology. What the view insists on is awareness: every supplied decision is one players are not practising, and one whose success cannot be read back as their own.
>
> **Player decisions are preserved selectively, not maximised.** If the aim is for players to recognise a moment or choose between options, the practice must leave that decision alive and contain what shows it. A whistle, a coach's shout or an unopposed run can remove it without anyone noticing. If the aim is doing rather than choosing, removing the decision can be exactly right.
>
> **Constraints are tools for changing the problem, not methods with guaranteed effects.** Their effects are real, specific, often unintended, vary between players and interact with each other. Players respond to the game actually created, not the lesson intended.
>
> **The player's role** varies with the practice: executing, coordinating, recognising, choosing. They are not required to discover everything; nor are they merely to comply.
>
> **Learning is treated with humility.** Behaviour in practice shows what the practice produces. Compliance is not understanding; success under a supplied condition is not choice; the same behaviour after the support is removed, in the same session, is an observation, not proof. Whether anything persists and transfers is, for almost every tactical manipulation studied, unknown.
>
> **The single question that carries this into coaching** is: *what am I telling them, and what am I leaving them to solve?*
>
> **Limits.** This account says how to build and read a practice. It does not yet say how the coach should teach within it (telling, asking, showing, stopping), how to judge learning over a season, how skills are acquired, how practice should progress with age, or what is worth teaching. Its strongest claims are about inference, not about what produces learning.

## 28. Alternative interpretation (attack)

**Strongest case that Phase 1 has NOT produced a coaching philosophy:**

1. **It has no ends.** A philosophy says what coaching is for and what players should become. Phase 1 takes objectives as given and is tactically neutral by design.
2. **It has no pedagogy.** It deferred exactly the part (how the coach teaches) that distinguishes one coaching philosophy from another in practice.
3. **Its values are epistemic.** "Don't over-infer", "know what you supplied", "no method is good apart from its purpose" are rules of **reasoning**. A coach who lectures constantly and one who never speaks can both follow them.
4. **Its only substantive design stance is conditional.** GI-2 says: *if* recognition is the aim, preserve it. It never says recognition **should** be the aim. The implied preference for understanding comes from the **product's aspirations**, not from Phase 1.
5. **It prescribes almost nothing** across the ten-activity test (§22); it classifies and reads.
6. **Therefore it sits above philosophies**, as a shared grammar for practice design and a discipline for reading evidence, compatible with several very different ways of coaching.

**Comparison (not decided by which sounds better):**

| | Philosophy reading (§27) | Framework reading (§28) |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts for Phase 1's values | Yes | Yes, as epistemic values |
| Accounts for tactical neutrality | Treats it as a virtue | Treats it as evidence of framework status |
| Accounts for pedagogical silence | Lists it as a limit | Treats it as disqualifying |
| Accounts for conditional principles | As a stance ("method follows purpose") | As a decision procedure without a purpose of its own |
| Risk if wrong | Overclaims: markets a philosophy that can't tell coaches how to teach | Underclaims: misses that consistent values already constrain practice |

**Assessment:** the framework reading explains **more of the evidence with fewer assumptions**, particularly the pedagogical silence and the absence of ends. The philosophy reading is accurate about the **values** but has to import the aim of "understanding" from outside Phase 1.

## 30. Missing pieces (ranked by logical dependency)

1. **Objectives: what is worth developing** (game understanding, game model, player development aims). Every conditional principle needs an objective as input; without this, the framework has nothing to apply to. **First by dependency.**
2. **COACH INTERVENTION**: how the coach teaches within the practice (tell, ask, show, cue, stop, feedback). Completes the means; likely the "missing half".
3. **Learning, retention and transfer indicators**: how a coach can reasonably judge whether anything is sticking. Needed before the north star's last clause is defensible; depends on 1 (what to look for) and interacts with 2.
4. **Progression / scaffolding over time**: how support is added and withdrawn across sessions. Depends on 2 and 3.
5. **Technical skill acquisition**: how execution is best learned. Largely independent; needed for the beginner case (§22).
6. **Age and development**: moderates 2–5; cannot be meaningfully addressed before them.
7. **Season planning**: depends on 1, 3 and 4.
8. **Motivation / psychology environment**: adjacent; Phase 1 placed sanctions and rewards outside, and they can stay outside a practice framework while mattering to a philosophy.

**Not all of these need to become research phases.** Some (1) may be partly **value choices** the product must make, informed but not settled by evidence.

## 31. Pass-1 verdict

### **C — Phase 1 has produced a practice-design and decision framework, not a coaching philosophy.**

**Why C and not B:**
- The parts that make a coaching philosophy distinctive (**what coaching is for**, **how the coach teaches**, **how learning is judged**) are respectively **unresearched**, **deferred** and **unsupported by available evidence**.
- The values Phase 1 does hold are overwhelmingly **epistemic and conditional**; they constrain **how to reason**, not **what to believe about coaching**.
- The ten-activity test (§22) shows the framework **classifies and reads** every activity without contradiction and **prescribes** almost nothing about teaching.

**Why it is close to B:**
- The framework carries a recognisable **stance**: purpose first, deliberate design, awareness of what is supplied, selective preservation of decisions, humility about learning, no method good apart from its purpose (§20).
- If PáircVision later **chooses its objectives** (missing piece 1) and completes **COACH INTERVENTION** (missing piece 2), this stance could become the core of a philosophy. Phase 1 would then be its **design-and-inference half**.

**What PáircVision can honestly say now [INTERPRETATION]:** it has a rigorous way to **build practices and read them**, which could sit beneath more than one coaching philosophy. It does **not** yet have a coaching philosophy of its own.
