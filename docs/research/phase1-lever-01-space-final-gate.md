# PáircVision Phase 1 — Lever 01 SPACE: Final Evidence & Falsification Gate

**Parent document:** [`phase1-lever-01-space.md`](./phase1-lever-01-space.md) (v1 evidence review)
**Type:** Verification and falsification gate. Research only: no drills, sessions, UI or software.
**Status:** Gate v1, September 2026

> **Rule of this document:** where a behaviour is *produced* during a constrained game, we say **"produced"** or **"appeared"**. We say **"learned"** only when retention (after a delay) or transfer (to an unconstrained game or to competition) has been measured. Across all the SPACE evidence reviewed here, that has almost never been done.

---

## 0. Method and verification limits

- **Scope.** A targeted search for **contradicting** as well as supporting evidence on the 10 working propositions and the 10 external claims. This is not a second broad review.
- **Access limits (stated honestly).** The research environment's network proxy blocked full-text access to PubMed/PMC, Frontiers, ResearchGate, Ovid/JSCR, England Football Learning, Hockey Canada, TU Dublin and MTU repositories. Verification was therefore done through search-engine indexes of the publisher and repository pages, i.e. abstracts, metadata and indexed page text. Where a detail rests only on that, it is marked **(abstract-level)**. Nothing below claims more than the abstract or indexed page supports.
- **Evidence classes.** The same classes as v1 are used: **[R]** peer-reviewed research, **[C]** conference abstract or thesis (not full peer review), **[GB]** governing-body guidance, **[PR]** practitioner resource, **[IND]** industry or federation analytics, **[TH]** theory, **[H]** PáircVision hypothesis.
- **Upgrade rule.** Practitioner and governing-body sources inform coaching practice. They are **never** upgraded to scientific evidence here, even when the body describes its own guidance as "evidence-based".

## 3. The 10 working propositions — verdicts

Verdict scale: **SUPPORTED** · **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** · **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** · **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS** · **CONTRADICTED**

### P1. Space is a probability lever, not a prescription → **SUPPORTED**

- **For:**
  - Every spatial study reports shifts in the *frequency or magnitude* of behaviours, never a deterministic outcome.
  - Effects are moderated by age (Castellano et al., 2016, U12 vs U13; Olthof et al., 2018), skill (Silva et al., 2014) and playing level (Malone et al., 2016 vs Mangan et al., 2019).
  - Session-to-session variability is high even when the "same" game is repeated (Dudley et al., 2024, rugby union).
- **Against / challenge:** "Probability lever" can **understate** some effects. Physical load responds to area very reliably. The Clemente et al. (2023) meta-analysis of 41 soccer studies found larger pitches increased total distance (ES ≈ 1.95), HSR (ES ≈ 1.20), surface area (ES ≈ 1.54) and stretch index (ES ≈ 1.02). For load, space is close to a dependable dial. For **tactical behaviour and learning**, it is genuinely probabilistic.
- **Refined wording:** *"Changing space changes how likely certain actions are. It does not guarantee them, and it guarantees nothing about learning. Physical load is the most predictable part."*

### P2. Relative space/density matters, but area-per-player alone cannot describe the practice → **SUPPORTED**

- **For:**
  - The same ApP with different player numbers does **not** produce equivalent responses (Castellano et al., 2016, *PLoS ONE*: 7, 9 and 11-a-side × 100, 200 and 300 m²/player gave different demands, and differently by age).
  - Shape changes team behaviour at similar areas (see P3).
  - Scoring-target configuration changes shape and dispersion at equal area (Castellano et al., 2016, *J Hum Kinet*; Travassos et al., 2014).
  - In Gaelic football, identical dimensions produced different load patterns at different playing levels.
- **Against:** none found. The dispute is about **which** ApP reference to use (full pitch vs effective playing space; Fradua et al., 2013 vs Olthof et al., 2019). It is not about whether ApP is sufficient.
- **Refined wording:** *"Area per player is a starting point, not a description. Shape, numbers, targets, rules and who is playing all change what the space means."*

### P3. Shape matters independently of total area → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**

- **For:**
  - Castellano, Fernández, Echeazarra, Barreira & Garganta (2017), *Anales de Psicología* 33(3):486–496: in 7-a-side youth games, varying **length only** (30–60 m, width fixed at 40 m) increased team length, convex hull and stretch index, while team width barely changed. Effects were larger in U13 than U14 (abstract-level).
  - González-Rodenas et al. (2021), *IJERPH* 18(19):10500: different pitch configurations changed playing tactics. "Long" and "wide" configurations produced more counterattacks, more offensive penetration and more goal-scoring opportunities than the standard format (abstract-level; variable-level contrasts between long and wide need full-text confirmation).
  - Basketball: widening the court by 1 m per side increased offensive dispersion, while defenders stayed organised around fixed spatial references such as the lane, the basket and the 3-point line (*Cuadernos de Psicología del Deporte*; abstract-level).
- **Against / complication:**
  - Frencken et al. (2013) found a **crossover** effect: length manipulations produced lateral changes and width manipulations produced longitudinal changes. That contradicts the simple "shape maps onto the matching team dimension" reading supported by Castellano et al. (2017).
  - **Most shape studies also change total area**, because lengthening a pitch enlarges it. Very few hold area constant while changing the length-to-width ratio. So "independently of total area" is **not yet cleanly demonstrated**.
- **Refined wording:** *"Shape seems to matter in its own right, but most studies change the size at the same time. Expect shape to change how teams stretch, and check what actually happens."*

### P4. Useful space is relational and dynamic, not merely empty grass → **SUPPORTED** (as a description of performance)

- **For:**
  - Dyadic research shows that decisions and outcomes are governed by *relative* distances and closing speeds (Passos et al., 2008, rugby; Correia et al., 2011; Vilar et al., 2013, futsal).
  - Local numerical dominance relates to scoring opportunities (Vilar, Araújo, Davids & Bar-Yam, 2013).
  - Space-control and "dominant region" models from tracking data value space by who can reach it first, not by its emptiness (Rein, Raabe & Memmert, 2017, *Human Movement Science*, pass effectiveness via space control; Fernández & Bornn, 2018, MIT Sloan conference paper **[C]**).
- **Against:** none found as a description. **Open question:** whether *coaches or players* can be taught to perceive "useful space" better. That is untested.
- **Refined wording:** *"Useful space is space your team can reach and use before the opposition closes it. Empty grass far from the play is often not useful."*

### P5. Movement away from the ball can create greater collective value than movement toward it → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** that it *can*; the comparative "**greater**" claim is a **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC / PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**

- **For:**
  - Fernández & Bornn (2018) [C] separate *space occupation* (space you create for yourself) from *space generation* (space you create for teammates by dragging opponents) and show that off-ball movement can be quantified.
  - Sherwood, Masters & Smith (2018), *IJSSC*: an observational study of 260 Super Rugby scrums with decoy runners, examining decoys' influence on defensive performance [R] (abstract-level; the direction and size of effects need full-text confirmation).
  - NBA "Gravity" [IND]: off-ball shooters draw defenders.
- **Against / challenge:**
  - **No study compares the collective value of movement away from the ball with movement towards it.**
  - Supporting close to the ball carrier is essential in many contexts, especially under pressure and in hand-pass-dominated Gaelic football.
  - Analytics valuations are model-dependent.
  - We found nothing on this in GAA.
- **Refined wording:** *"Movement away from the ball can drag defenders and open space for a teammate. Sometimes that helps more than coming short; sometimes coming short is exactly right."*

### P6. Creating space for another player is itself a meaningful game action → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED**

- **For:** It is measurable (space-generation metrics, decoy analyses), and the mechanism (defenders are drawn to threats) is well grounded in the dyad and interpersonal-distance research.
- **Against / limits:**
  - Its link to team outcomes is shown mainly in conference or industry analytics, not in peer-reviewed outcome studies.
  - It is invisible in event data, including the event-level match data PáircVision already collects.
  - It is untested in GAA.
- **Refined wording:** *"A run that pulls a defender away so a teammate gets the ball in space is a real contribution, even though it never appears on a stats sheet."*

### P7. Prefer constraints that make a behaviour *useful* over rules that make it *compulsory* → **PARTIALLY SUPPORTED** (converging indirect evidence; no head-to-head learning test)

- **For:**
  - Gonçalves et al. (2017, *JSCR*): mandatory positional zones "greatly impaired the coadaptation between teammates' positioning" and reduced physical output, compared with free spacing.
  - *Frontiers in Psychology* (2019) 10:1554, U15/U17 SSCGs: "increasing rules manipulations appeared to negatively impact on teams' exploratory behaviour" (abstract-level; authors to confirm).
  - Motor-learning **guidance hypothesis** (Salmoni, Schmidt & Walter, 1984): heavy guidance improves acquisition performance but can create dependency, and performance drops when the guidance is removed.
  - Woods, Dowsett & Keogh (2025, *Sports Medicine – Open*; systematic review, 9 studies): nonlinear pedagogy (which favours affordance-based constraints) was better in **≈66% of tactical outcomes** in the studies synthesised descriptively.
- **Against / challenge:**
  - The same review found **no significant difference for most outcomes**, including most technical ones. Evidence quality was limited, and the assessment instruments were not always representative.
  - No study compares a "useful" constraint with a "compulsory" rule **for the same target behaviour with retention and transfer tests**.
  - Compulsory rules (e.g., "must pass before scoring") are ubiquitous and may be legitimate for brief *exposure*.
- **Refined wording:** *"Where you can, set the game up so the behaviour you want actually pays off, rather than making it a rule. Rules get compliance quickly, but may not teach players when the behaviour is the right choice."*

### P8. Avoid constraints that systematically punish genuinely good decisions → **PRACTITIONER HEURISTIC** (theory-consistent; not directly tested)

- **For:**
  - England Football's own guidance warns that long, narrow spaces can make players play forward "too quickly or ignore the benefits of backward passes". That is a governing body recognising the risk [GB].
  - Representative learning design theory holds that practice should keep functional decision–information couplings (Pinder et al., 2011) [TH].
  - The 2025 *J Sports Sci Med* study found lower decision-making quality on the smallest pitches [R, one study, acute].
- **Against / challenge:**
  - TGfU "exaggeration" and differential learning **deliberately** disrupt dominant solutions. Temporarily making a good option less available can be a legitimate design choice.
  - The key word is **"systematically"**: persistently, without release, and without the player understanding why.
  - Nothing tests whether such constraints harm learning.
- **Refined wording:** *"If your rule keeps punishing a decision that would be right in a real match, only keep it for a short spell, then remove it and see what players choose."*

### P9. Behaviour appearing during a constrained game is not evidence it has been learned or will transfer → **SUPPORTED**

- **For:**
  - The learning–performance distinction (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015) [R, high].
  - The guidance hypothesis [R].
  - SSG *intervention* meta-analysis (Clemente et al., 2021, *Frontiers in Psychology*): SSG programmes improved **technical execution** (moderate within-group effect), but tactical behaviour was too rarely measured to conclude anything.
  - We found no invasion-sport study that isolates space, removes it and tests transfer.
- **Caveat (symmetry):** absence of evidence of transfer is **not** evidence of *no* transfer. The honest statement is "unknown", not "doesn't work".
- **Refined wording:** *"Seeing it in the practice game tells you the game is producing it. Whether players have learned it only shows when the rule is gone, days later, and in matches."*

### P10. EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN → **PÁIRCVISION HYPOTHESIS**

- **Consistent with:**
  - Newell's stages (coordination → control → skill).
  - The ecological-dynamics "search → discover → exploit" description (Davids, Button & Bennett, 2008).
  - Skill-acquisition periodisation frameworks (Farrow & Robertson, 2017; Otte et al., 2019, PoST).
  - Fading guidance (the guidance hypothesis).
  - Retention-testing conventions in motor learning.
- **Not tested:** no study evaluates this sequence, or any comparable sequence, for spatial constraints.
- **Challenges:**
  1. **RELEASE within the same session measures immediate after-effects, not retention.** RETAIN needs a delay (≥24 h, ideally a week or more).
  2. **EXPOSE** could be implemented as a compulsory rule, which is in tension with P7. It must be phrased as "make the behaviour available and useful", not "force it".
  3. Learning is non-linear, and players may cycle between stages.
  4. Without a stable reference game and a baseline, RELEASE and RETAIN observations cannot be interpreted.
- **Refined wording:** see §F. Present it as **a way of organising a coach's observations**, not a proven learning sequence.

## 5. Decisions: *more*, *faster* and *better* are different things

PáircVision must keep five separate concepts apart:

| Concept | Definition | What small spaces do (acute evidence) | How it could be measured |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Decision opportunities** (frequency) | Number of on-ball decision points per player per minute | ↑ (more involvements; [R] moderate) | Event counts |
| **Decision time / speed** | Time available or taken between receiving and acting | Likely ↓ time available (shorter interpersonal distances; inferred) | Video timing, tracking |
| **Decision quality** (appropriateness) | Whether the chosen option fits the affordances present (e.g., GPAI-style "appropriate decision" coding; Oslin, Mitchell & Griffin, 1998) | **May ↓** on the smallest pitches ([R], one 2025 study; correlated with physiological strain) | Expert coding against criteria |
| **Execution** | Whether the chosen action succeeded technically | Mixed: passing success ↓ and dribbling success ↑ on small pitches (one study) | Success / error coding |
| **Decision learning** | A lasting change in decision quality, shown in retention or transfer | **Unknown.** No spatial-isolation study measured it | Pre / post / delayed tests in a reference game or matches |

**Firm rule:** the England Football phrase "make quick decisions" and the external claim "reducing space improves/accelerates decision-making" are about **opportunities and speed**. Neither is evidence for **quality** or **learning**, and PáircVision must not merge them.

## A. What PáircVision can responsibly teach a coach today

1. **Bigger areas usually mean more running and less contact. Smaller areas usually mean more touches, tackles and scrambles.** This is well established in soccer, supported in hurling and elite Gaelic football, but **not always** true in Gaelic football (one sub-elite study found running peaked on the medium pitch).
2. **Area per player is a starting point, not the whole story.** The same area per player with different numbers, shapes or targets produces different games.
3. **Useful space is space your team can reach before the opposition can close it.** It is relational and changes every second.
4. **What you see in the practice game is what the game is *producing*.** It is not proof players have *learned* it.
5. **Rigidly fixing players into zones can make teammates worse at adjusting to each other.** One good study shows this; treat it as a warning, not a law.
6. **Scoring targets shape where teams spread.** More or wider targets pull teams wider (acute evidence).
7. **More decisions and quicker decisions are not the same as better decisions.** Small spaces give more and quicker decisions; there is some evidence they can lower decision quality.
8. **Children usually get more involvement and enjoy scaled-down formats more.** Whether that produces better long-term players is less certain.

## B. What PáircVision may present only as a coaching option or hypothesis

1. Long/narrow spaces may bias forward play and short/wide spaces may bias width and switching (**practitioner heuristic**, with a known counter-finding).
2. Movement away from the ball can create more team value than coming short, *in some situations* (**heuristic / hypothesis**).
3. Making a behaviour *useful* rather than *compulsory* may give better understanding (**partially supported, not directly tested**).
4. Avoid rules that keep punishing decisions that would be right in a match (**practitioner heuristic**).
5. Match-proportioned space (≈370 m² per player in Gaelic games) may make practice more game-like tactically (**extrapolated from soccer**).
6. EXPOSE → EXPLORE → RELEASE → RETAIN as a way of **organising observation** (**PáircVision hypothesis**).
7. Temporary constraints followed by a standard reference game, and a later re-check, as a way for a coach to *look for* transfer (**defensible method only when done with baseline and delay**).
8. The 2025 GAA football rules as a real-world example of how spatial rules reshape a game (**observation, not evidence of learning**).

## C. What PáircVision should NOT claim

1. ❌ "Small spaces improve decision-making" or "small spaces make players decide better". Only *more / quicker* decisions are supported, acutely. The one direct study points the other way for quality.
2. ❌ "This spatial set-up **teaches** X" or "players **learned** X" on the basis of seeing X in the constrained game.
3. ❌ Any fixed "correct" pitch size or area per player for Gaelic football or hurling.
4. ❌ "Bigger pitch always means more high-speed running" in Gaelic football (Mangan et al., 2019 contradicts it).
5. ❌ "Wide pitch = team plays wider" as a certainty (the crossover effect).
6. ❌ That any evidence exists on *tactical* or *learning* effects of space **in Gaelic football or hurling specifically**. It does not.
7. ❌ Federation involvement figures (ice hockey "6× shots", etc.) presented as scientific evidence of learning.
8. ❌ That zones or corridors "create compliance, not understanding" as a *proven* fact. The acute co-adaptation cost is shown; understanding was never measured.
9. ❌ That movement away from the ball is *generally* more valuable than movement toward it.
10. ❌ Principles attributed to FIFA that were not located in FIFA primary material.

## D. Important contradictions

1. **Gaelic football, same pitch sizes, opposite running patterns.** Elite players ran most on the largest pitch (Malone et al., 2016); sub-elite players' HSR peaked on the medium pitch (Mangan et al., 2019). Candidate explanations are playing level, fitness, motivation or game rules. **Unresolved.**
2. **Shape effects: crossover vs direct mapping.** Frencken et al. (2013) found crossover effects; Castellano et al. (2017) found length mainly changing team length.
3. **"Small = better decisions" (practitioner) vs smaller fields → lower decision quality** (*J Sports Sci Med*, 2025).
4. **Which area per player is "representative"?** Full-pitch-derived (Olthof et al.) vs effective-playing-space-derived (Fradua et al.) give very different answers.
5. **Nonlinear pedagogy advantage vs no difference.** Tactical outcomes favour nonlinear approaches in the studies synthesised descriptively, but most outcomes show no difference (Woods et al., 2025).
6. **Federation messaging inconsistency.** Indexed Hockey Canada text contains both "twice" and "six times" as many shots.
7. **Scanning.** Small formats increase scanning *frequency*, but the scanning situations may be unrepresentative of the full game.

## E. Remaining research gaps

1. **No invasion-sport study isolates a spatial manipulation, removes it, and tests retention and transfer.** This is the defining gap.
2. **There are no tactical, positional or decision-quality studies of space in Gaelic football or hurling.** All GAA evidence is physical load plus technical counts.
3. Shape effects with **area held constant** (ratio-only designs).
4. **Head-to-head tests** of "useful" constraints vs "compulsory" rules for the same target behaviour.
5. **Decision quality** (not frequency) as an outcome of spatial manipulation, measured over weeks.
6. Peer-reviewed evidence on **off-ball space creation** and its link to scoring, especially in hand-pass / kick-pass codes.
7. **Children:** whether scaled space improves long-term skill, not just involvement. This is especially needed for GAA Go Games.
8. Validated, low-cost **transfer indicators** a coach could use without tracking data.
9. Evaluation of the **2025 GAA football rule changes** as a spatial natural experiment.
10. Whether **fading** spatial constraints (gradual vs abrupt removal) changes retention.

## F. Final recommended wording for the SPACE lever (plain coach language)

> **SPACE — changing the size and shape of the game**
>
> Changing the size and shape of your practice game changes **what is likely to happen**. It does not decide it.
>
> - **Make it smaller** and players will usually get **more touches, more tackles and less time**. Expect more scrambles and more mistakes, and possibly worse choices, not better ones.
> - **Make it bigger** and players will usually **run more and have more time**, with more passing options. Expect fewer players involved and less close pressure.
> - **Change the shape** (longer or wider) and the team's shape will change, but not always the way you expect, so watch.
> - **Move or add scoring targets** and you change where teams spread out.
>
> Good space is **not just empty grass**. It is space your team can get to and use before the opposition closes it. Sometimes the most useful run is **away** from the ball, pulling a defender and making room for a teammate.
>
> Try to set the game up so the thing you want **actually pays off**, rather than making it a rule. Be careful with rules that keep punishing a choice that would be right in a real match.
>
> **Most important:** when you see players doing it in your practice game, that tells you **the game is producing it**. It does not yet tell you they have **learned** it. To find out:
> 1. take the constraint away,
> 2. play your normal game,
> 3. look again next week,
> 4. and watch for it in matches.
>
> *Evidence note:* most of what we know comes from soccer and from short-term effects. In Gaelic football and hurling, research so far covers mainly running and fitness, and even that isn't fully consistent.
